We begin with a physical event: two light nuclei fuse and release energy. Each step
between that event and a working magnetic confinement system introduces a new demand.
Following those demands will give us the tokamak's components, the quantities that
describe them, and the equations that tie them together.

\section*{1. The first requirement: make nuclei approach}
The fuel considered here is an equal mixture of deuterium and tritium, the two heavy
isotopes of hydrogen. Their reaction is
\begin{equation}
 {}^{2}\mathrm H+{}^{3}\mathrm H\longrightarrow{}^{4}\mathrm{He}\;(3.5\,\mathrm{MeV})
 +n\;(14.1\,\mathrm{MeV}),\qquad Q=17.6\,\mathrm{MeV}.
 \label{eq:dt}
\end{equation}
The energy comes from the decrease in rest mass. In the centre-of-mass frame, the two
products carry equal and opposite momenta, so their kinetic energies are inversely
proportional to their masses. The neutron takes about four fifths of the released
energy; the helium nucleus, or alpha particle, takes the rest. That division will
later determine how much energy heats the fuel and how much must be collected outside it.

Both reacting nuclei carry charge $+e$. Their electrostatic repulsion is
\begin{equation}
 U(r)=\frac{e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0r}.
 \label{eq:coulomb}
\end{equation}
At a separation of $2\,\mathrm{fm}$ this is about $0.72\,\mathrm{MeV}$. Quantum
tunnelling allows fusion at much lower collision energies. At temperatures of order
$10$--$20\,\mathrm{keV}$, collisions in the energetic part of the ion distribution
have a useful probability of penetrating the barrier. We use temperature in energy
units throughout: $T$ denotes $k_BT_K$, so $10\,\mathrm{keV}$ corresponds to
$1.16\times10^8\,\mathrm K$.

Fuel at this temperature must be kept away from material surfaces. Contact would
remove its energy, erode the surface and introduce impurities into the fuel. A
\emph{vacuum vessel} provides an evacuated chamber that limits contact with cold gas;
the hot fuel inside it must be confined by electromagnetic fields. Reaction energies
and the reactor-design framework used here follow Freidberg, Mangiarotti and
Minervini.\footnote{J.~P.~Freidberg, F.~J.~Mangiarotti and J.~Minervini,
``Designing a Tokamak Fusion Reactor---How Does Plasma Physics Fit In?'' (2015),
\href{https://library.psfc.mit.edu/catalog/reports/2010/15ja/15ja021/15ja021_full.pdf}{open manuscript}.
This is the reference for the reduced reactor model developed later.}

\designconsequence{An evacuated vessel limits cold-gas contact with the hot fuel. Keeping the plasma clear of its wall requires magnetic confinement.}

\section*{2. Charged particles give us a handle}
At these temperatures the fuel is ionized: electrons and nuclei form a plasma.
On lengths large compared with its electrostatic screening length, the positive and
negative charge densities nearly cancel. This property is called \emph{quasineutrality}.
Each constituent still responds to the Lorentz force,
\begin{equation}
 m\frac{d\mathbf v}{dt}=q(\mathbf E+\mathbf v\times\mathbf B).
 \label{eq:lorentz}
\end{equation}
Set $\mathbf E=0$ and $\mathbf B=B\hat{\mathbf z}$. Since
$\mathbf v\cdot(\mathbf v\times\mathbf B)=0$, the magnetic force does no work: it preserves the particle's
kinetic energy while turning its velocity. With $w=v_x+iv_y$, the perpendicular
motion satisfies $\dot w=-i(qB/m)w$. Thus $w=w(0)e^{-iqBt/m}$, and integration gives
\begin{equation}
 \omega_c=\frac{|q|B}{m},\qquad
 r_L=\frac{v_\perp}{\omega_c}=\frac{mv_\perp}{|q|B}.
 \label{eq:gyro}
\end{equation}
Here $\omega_c$ is the gyrofrequency and $r_L$ the gyroradius. Parallel motion remains
free, so the orbit is a helix around a magnetic field line. A $10\,\mathrm{keV}$
deuteron with $v_\perp=\sqrt{2T/m_D}=9.8\times10^5\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ has
$r_L=6.8\,\mathrm{mm}$ in a $3\,\mathrm T$ field. A metre-scale chamber can therefore
contain small transverse orbits. Its next problem is escape along the field.

\designconsequence{A magnetic field bounds transverse motion while leaving particles free to stream along it. Confinement must therefore address parallel escape.}

\section*{3. A straight magnetic corridor}
A coil is a conductor wound into turns so that their magnetic fields add. For a long
solenoid, the turns surround a cylindrical volume. Amp\`ere's law gives
\begin{equation}
 \oint_C\mathbf B\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell=\mu_0I_{\rm linked}.
 \label{eq:ampere}
\end{equation}
Choose a rectangular path with length $L$ inside the solenoid and its return far
outside. \samplemodel{For a solenoid long compared with its radius, the exterior field is small.} With $n_{\rm turn}$ turns per unit length, each carrying current $I_c$,
\begin{equation}
 BL=\mu_0(n_{\rm turn}L)I_c,\qquad B\simeq\mu_0n_{\rm turn}I_c.
 \label{eq:solenoid}
\end{equation}
This field guides particles down the tube. At $v_\parallel\sim10^6\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$,
a particle traverses a $10\,\mathrm m$ device in about $10\,\mu\mathrm s$.
Increasing $B$ reduces the gyroradius but leaves this parallel transit time unchanged.
Magnetic mirrors can reflect a range of particle pitch angles; here we pursue another
way to remove direct end loss: join the ends of the corridor.

\designconsequence{The straight solenoid leaves a direct escape path at each end. Closing that path requires a different geometry.}

\section*{4. Closing the corridor creates the torus}
The resulting tube is a torus. Its long-way direction is \emph{toroidal}; the short
way around a tube cross-section is \emph{poloidal}. The major radius $R_0$ measures
from the symmetry axis to the cross-section's centre. The minor radius $a$ measures
the horizontal extent of the plasma from that centre. Their ratio is
\begin{equation}
 A\equiv\frac{R_0}{a}\qquad\text{(aspect ratio)}.
 \label{eq:aspect}
\end{equation}
Toroidal-field (TF) coils wrap around the tube at successive positions around the
ring. Approximating them by a continuous, axisymmetric winding, a circular
Amp\`ere path of radius $R$ links $N$ turns carrying $I_c$:
\begin{equation}
 2\pi RB_\varphi(R)=\mu_0NI_c,\qquad
 B_\varphi(R)=\frac{\mu_0NI_c}{2\pi R}.
 \label{eq:btor}
\end{equation}
\samplemodel{The continuous-winding approximation is axisymmetric. Discrete TF coils introduce a periodic field variation called \emph{ripple}.} The field is stronger on the
\emph{inboard} side, nearest the symmetry axis, than on the \emph{outboard} side:
\begin{equation}
 \frac{B_{\rm in}}{B_{\rm out}}=\frac{R_0+a}{R_0-a}=\frac{A+1}{A-1}.
 \label{eq:fieldratio}
\end{equation}
The closed path removes the ends, but its curvature and field gradient change the
particle orbits. We must calculate that change before accepting the magnetic bottle.

\designconsequence{Closing the corridor creates field curvature and a gradient, $B_\varphi\propto1/R$. Their effect on particle orbits must now be checked.}

\section*{5. Why the toroidal bottle still leaks}
When $r_L/R_0\ll1$, we can average over gyromotion and follow the orbit's centre,
called the \emph{guiding centre}. For a slowly varying perpendicular force $\mathbf F$,
seek a constant drift velocity satisfying
$q\mathbf v_F\times\mathbf B+\mathbf F=0$. Crossing with $\mathbf B$ yields
\begin{equation}
 \mathbf v_F=\frac{\mathbf F\times\mathbf B}{qB^2}.
 \label{eq:forcedrift}
\end{equation}
Two effective forces arise in a toroidal field. The magnetic-moment magnitude of a
gyro-orbit follows from its circulating charge:
\[
 \mu=\frac{|q|\omega_c}{2\pi}\,\pi r_L^2=\frac{mv_\perp^2}{2B}.
\]
For fields varying slowly over a gyroperiod and gyroradius, $\mu$ is an adiabatic
invariant. The perpendicular energy $\mu B$ then gives a gradient force
$-\mu\nabla B$. Following a curved field line also gives the effective outward
curvature force $(mv_\parallel^2/R)\hat{\mathbf R}$. The guiding-centre ordering
underlying these expressions is developed systematically by Brizard and Hahm.\footnote{
A.~J.~Brizard and T.~S.~Hahm, ``Foundations of nonlinear gyrokinetic theory,''
\emph{Reviews of Modern Physics} \textbf{79}, 421--468 (2007),
\href{https://doi.org/10.1103/RevModPhys.79.421}{doi:10.1103/RevModPhys.79.421}.}

Because $B\propto1/R$, we have $\nabla B=-(B/R)\hat{\mathbf R}$.
Both effective forces point outward. With $\mathbf B=B\hat{\boldsymbol\varphi}$
and $\hat{\mathbf R}\times\hat{\boldsymbol\varphi}=\hat{\mathbf Z}$,
\begin{equation}
 \mathbf v_{\nabla B}=\frac{mv_\perp^2}{2qBR}\hat{\mathbf Z},\qquad
 \mathbf v_c=\frac{mv_\parallel^2}{qBR}\hat{\mathbf Z},\qquad
 \mathbf v_d=\frac{m}{qBR}\left(v_\parallel^2+\frac{v_\perp^2}{2}\right)\hat{\mathbf Z}.
 \label{eq:toroidaldrift}
\end{equation}
Ions and electrons drift vertically in opposite directions. The resulting charge
separation creates a vertical electric field. Setting $\mathbf F=q\mathbf E$ in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:forcedrift} gives
\begin{equation}
 \mathbf v_E=\frac{\mathbf E\times\mathbf B}{B^2}.
 \label{eq:exb}
\end{equation}
This drift is common to both species and carries the plasma outward toward the wall.
A purely toroidal field therefore leaves a systematic escape mechanism.

The remedy is to make field lines visit the top and bottom of the tube. Then a
vertical drift alternates between outward and inward motion relative to the local
magnetic surface. Adding a poloidal field provides this rotation and allows bounded
orbit excursions in the axisymmetric confinement picture.

\designconsequence{Toroidal drifts separate charge and drive outward motion. A poloidal field is needed to carry particles around the cross-section.}

\section*{6. Plasma current supplies the second field}
External helical windings can produce the required twist; that is the stellarator
route. The tokamak uses a toroidal current in the plasma to generate the principal
poloidal field. For a circular cross-section at large aspect ratio, Amp\`ere's law gives
\begin{equation}
 B_\theta(r)\simeq\frac{\mu_0I_{\rm enc}(r)}{2\pi r},
 \label{eq:bpol}
\end{equation}
where $I_{\rm enc}(r)$ is the plasma current inside radius $r$. The combination
$\mathbf B=B_\varphi\hat{\boldsymbol\varphi}+B_\theta\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}$
produces helical field lines.

To initiate that current, place a solenoid through the central hole and change its
magnetic flux. Faraday's law,
\begin{equation}
 \oint\mathbf E\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell=-\frac{d\Phi}{dt},
 \label{eq:faraday}
\end{equation}
creates a toroidal electric field. The central solenoid is the primary winding of a
transformer; the plasma ring acts as its one-turn secondary. This gives the
\emph{plasma current} $I_p$ a physical source. The TF coils provide the long-way
field, and the induced plasma current provides the short-way field.

\designconsequence{Changing central-solenoid flux drives plasma current and its poloidal field. The resulting helical field needs a measure of its pitch.}

\section*{7. The helix has a pitch: the safety factor}
A field-line displacement is parallel to $\mathbf B$. On a circular surface its
toroidal and poloidal lengths therefore satisfy
\begin{equation}
 \frac{R\,d\varphi}{r\,d\theta}=\frac{B_\varphi}{B_\theta}.
 \label{eq:pitch}
\end{equation}
At large aspect ratio take $R\simeq R_0$ and $B_\varphi\simeq B_0$. During one
poloidal circuit the field line advances toroidally through $2\pi q$, where
\begin{equation}
 q(r)\equiv\frac{\Delta\varphi}{2\pi}\simeq\frac{rB_0}{R_0B_\theta(r)}.
 \label{eq:qdef}
\end{equation}
The \emph{safety factor} $q$ counts toroidal turns per poloidal turn. Its historical
name reflects its use in stability analysis. Substituting Eq.~\eqref{eq:bpol} gives
\begin{equation}
 q(r)\simeq\frac{2\pi r^2B_0}{\mu_0R_0I_{\rm enc}(r)},\qquad
 q_a\simeq\frac{2\pi a^2B_0}{\mu_0R_0I_p}.
 \label{eq:qedge}
\end{equation}
Thus an imposed lower bound on the edge safety factor becomes a ceiling on current:
\begin{equation}
 I_p\simeq\frac{2\pi a^2B_0}{\mu_0R_0q_a}.
 \label{eq:ipq}
\end{equation}
Increasing plasma current strengthens the poloidal field and reduces the number of
toroidal turns per poloidal circuit. Size, current, field and pitch are now coupled.
For $q=m/n$ in lowest terms, the ideal field line closes after $n$ poloidal turns and
$m$ toroidal turns. An irrational $q$ produces a line that densely samples its surface.

\section*{8. The plasma pushes back: pressure and beta}
Electrons and ions both contribute to pressure. With singly charged fuel,
$n_e\simeq n_i\equiv n$, and equal temperatures,
\begin{equation}
 p=n_eT_e+n_iT_i\simeq2nT.
 \label{eq:pressure}
\end{equation}
At $n=10^{20}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}$ and $T=10\,\mathrm{keV}$ this gives
$p\simeq3.2\times10^5\,\mathrm{Pa}$. The pressure gradient drives expansion.
On scales large compared with particle orbits, a single-fluid model with scalar
pressure gives
\begin{equation}
 \rho_m\frac{d\mathbf u}{dt}=-\nabla p+\mathbf j\times\mathbf B.
 \label{eq:mhdmomentum}
\end{equation}
Here $\rho_m$ is mass density, $\mathbf u$ bulk velocity and $\mathbf j$ current
per unit area. We use static magnetohydrodynamic (MHD) equilibrium: negligible bulk
acceleration, isotropic pressure and negligible viscous force. Then
\begin{equation}
 \mathbf j\times\mathbf B=\nabla p.
 \label{eq:forcebalance}
\end{equation}
Dotting with $\mathbf B$ immediately gives
\begin{equation}
 \mathbf B\cdot\nabla p=0.
 \label{eq:pressurefieldline}
\end{equation}
Pressure is constant along each field line, and on a nested surface densely sampled
by those lines. Magnetic geometry must support this pressure distribution.

A natural pressure scale follows from the work needed to establish a field. For a
long solenoid of $N$ turns, length $L$ and area $S$, its linked flux is $NBS$ and
$B=\mu_0NI_c/L$. Integrating $dW_B=I_c\,d(NBS)$ gives
\begin{equation}
 W_B=\frac{B^2}{2\mu_0}SL.
 \label{eq:fieldenergy}
\end{equation}
The energy density $B^2/(2\mu_0)$ has pressure units. Its ratio to plasma pressure
defines the dimensionless parameter
\begin{equation}
 \beta\equiv\frac{p}{B^2/(2\mu_0)}.
 \label{eq:beta}
\end{equation}
Beta measures how much plasma pressure is supported relative to the magnetic energy
density. Later global estimates use representative or volume-averaged pressure and
$B_0$, with that convention stated in the calculation. The allowable beta requires a
stability model; the definition itself sets no universal limit.

\designconsequence{Plasma pressure and current produce forces that require external equilibrium fields. The next task is to identify the field that balances radial expansion.}

\section*{9. External coils hold the plasma in position}
The plasma ring's pressure and self-generated magnetic field create an outward
expansion tendency. A vertical field supplies an adjustable radial force on its
toroidal current. For a ring element,
\begin{equation}
 d\mathbf F=I_pR_0\,d\varphi\,\hat{\boldsymbol\varphi}\times
 B_v\hat{\mathbf Z}=I_pR_0B_v\,d\varphi\,\hat{\mathbf R}.
 \label{eq:ringforce}
\end{equation}
Changing the sign of $B_v$ reverses the local force. The circumferentially integrated
\emph{radial load} is $2\pi R_0I_pB_v$; vector forces around a complete symmetric
ring cancel in translation while still expanding or compressing the ring.

External \emph{poloidal-field coils}, or PF coils, are circular windings around the
symmetry axis, placed at selected radii and heights. Their fields lie in the
$R$--$Z$ plane. By changing their currents we can supply the vertical field that
balances radial expansion, and later adjust plasma height and shape. A power supply
is the circuit that controls each coil's current; the coil is the magnetic actuator.
The necessary field magnitude depends on the plasma pressure and current profiles,
which enter the equilibrium equation derived below.

The coil roles are now distinct. TF coils generate $B_\varphi$. The central solenoid
induces $I_p$, whose field supplies the main poloidal twist. The external PF system
positions and shapes the resulting plasma.

\designconsequence{A controlled vertical field supplies radial force on the plasma ring. PF coils must control its cross-sectional boundary as well as its position.}

\section*{10. Flux surfaces define the magnetic boundary}
In cylindrical coordinates $(R,\varphi,Z)$, axisymmetry means
$\partial/\partial\varphi=0$. The divergence-free condition becomes
\begin{equation}
 \partial_R(RB_R)+\partial_Z(RB_Z)=0.
 \label{eq:divfree}
\end{equation}
Introduce a flux function $\psi(R,Z)$ through
\begin{equation}
 B_R=-\frac1R\partial_Z\psi,\qquad B_Z=\frac1R\partial_R\psi,\qquad
 \mathbf B_p=\frac{\nabla\psi\times\hat{\boldsymbol\varphi}}R.
 \label{eq:psidef}
\end{equation}
The mixed derivatives cancel in Eq.~\eqref{eq:divfree}. Also,
\begin{equation}
 \mathbf B\cdot\nabla\psi=0,
 \label{eq:psiconstant}
\end{equation}
so field lines lie on surfaces of constant $\psi$. With this convention, $2\pi$
times a difference in $\psi$ is a poloidal magnetic-flux difference. Rotating a closed
contour of $\psi$ about the symmetry axis constructs a magnetic surface.

The outermost closed surface defines the confined plasma boundary. The vacuum vessel
surrounds this magnetic configuration. PF-coil currents and plasma current together
set its flux contours; controlling these currents lets us change the boundary's
position and shape. The point inside the nested contours about which they contract
is the \emph{magnetic axis}.



This flux description is the starting point for the Grad--Shafranov equilibrium
calculation in §§21--24. An analytic treatment is given by Cerfon and
Freidberg.\footnote{A.~J.~Cerfon and J.~P.~Freidberg, ``One size fits all'' analytic
solutions to the Grad--Shafranov equation, \emph{Physics of Plasmas} \textbf{17},
032502 (2010), \href{https://doi.org/10.1063/1.3328818}{doi:10.1063/1.3328818}.}

\section*{11. The transformer has a finite flux budget}
Faraday's law around the plasma ring defines the loop voltage:
\begin{equation}
 V_{\rm loop}\equiv\oint\mathbf E\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell
 =-\frac{d\Phi_{\rm link}}{dt}.
 \label{eq:loopvoltage}
\end{equation}
Over a discharge,
\begin{equation}
 \int V_{\rm loop}\,dt=-\Delta\Phi_{\rm link}.
 \label{eq:voltseconds}
\end{equation}
One volt-second equals one weber of linked flux. The central solenoid can swing only
through a finite flux range before reaching its current, field or mechanical limits.
The flux budget pays for initiating and ramping the plasma current and for maintaining
it against resistance. Sustained transformer-driven operation therefore has a finite
pulse length. Longer operation needs another source of current or a new pulse after
resetting the solenoid.

Electrical resistance also provides \emph{ohmic heating}. In a resistive fluid model,
\begin{equation}
 P_{\rm ohm}=\int\eta j^2\,dV\simeq I_p^2R_p,
 \label{eq:ohmic}
\end{equation}
where $\eta$ is resistivity and $R_p$ an effective plasma resistance. During a current
ramp, part of the electrical input changes magnetic energy. For a simple circuit with
fixed inductance $L_p$, $V_{\rm loop}=L_p\dot I_p+R_pI_p$; only when the inductive
term is small does $I_pV_{\rm loop}\simeq P_{\rm ohm}$.

In a fully ionized collisional plasma, classical Spitzer resistivity scales as
\begin{equation}
 \eta\propto T_e^{-3/2}
 \label{eq:spitzer}
\end{equation}
for fixed composition and approximately fixed Coulomb logarithm.\footnote{Naval
Research Laboratory, \emph{NRL Plasma Formulary} (2019), collisional transport and
resistivity relations, \href{https://www.nrl.navy.mil/Portals/38/PDF\%20Files/NRL_Plasma_Formulary_2019.pdf}{open formulary}.}
At comparable current density, heating falls as the electrons get hotter. Resistance
is useful during startup, but reaching fusion temperature calls for auxiliary heating.

\section*{12. Heating must balance energy loss}
A Maxwellian species has thermal energy density $3n_sT_s/2$. For approximately uniform
fuel at equal ion and electron temperatures,
\begin{equation}
 W\simeq3nTV,\qquad P_{\rm loss}\equiv W/\tau_E.
 \label{eq:taue}
\end{equation}
Here $\tau_E$ is the energy-confinement time. In this section $P_{\rm loss}$ includes
transport and radiation losses together; each loss channel is counted once. The
thermal-energy balance is
\begin{equation}
 \frac{dW}{dt}=P_{\rm ohm}+P_{\rm aux}+P_\alpha-P_{\rm loss}.
 \label{eq:powerbalance}
\end{equation}
The powers on the right are deposited in the plasma. Auxiliary heating must raise
$W$ against losses while fusion power is small. Later, confined alpha particles
from Eq.~\eqref{eq:dt} transfer their energy to the fuel, providing $P_\alpha$.
Neutrons carry their energy out of the magnetically confined plasma.

A \emph{neutral-beam injector} accelerates ions and neutralizes them before injection.
The fast neutral atoms cross the confining magnetic field, become ionized inside the
plasma, and transfer energy and momentum through collisions. A \emph{radio-frequency}
(RF) system instead sends electromagnetic waves through an antenna or launcher; suitable
frequencies and wave patterns transfer energy to electrons or ions. These systems
require access openings, or ports, through the vessel. With suitable injection or
wave conditions, they can also drive plasma current without transformer induction.

We now have the equipment that produces a hot current-carrying plasma. Its useful
volume and stable pressure depend on the shape that the PF system can create and hold.

\designconsequence{Beam injectors or RF launchers supply auxiliary heat through vessel ports. Their deposited power must cover losses and any increase in stored energy.}

\section*{13. Elongation buys volume}
An elliptical cross-section has horizontal semi-axis $a$ and vertical semi-axis
$\kappa a$, where
\begin{equation}
 \kappa\equiv\frac{\text{vertical semi-axis}}{\text{horizontal semi-axis}}
 \label{eq:kappa}
\end{equation}
is the \emph{elongation}. A convenient boundary parameterization is
$R=R_0+a\cos\theta$, $Z=\kappa a\sin\theta$. Its area is $\pi\kappa a^2$.
Rotating the ellipse about the symmetry axis gives
\begin{equation}
 V=2\pi R_0\,\pi\kappa a^2=2\pi^2R_0a^2\kappa.
 \label{eq:elongated-volume}
\end{equation}
\samplescope{The volume formula is exact for the specified ellipse with $R_0>a$.} At fixed $R_0$ and $a$,
increasing $\kappa$ increases volume in direct proportion. At a fixed plasma state,
that volume can contain more fuel and produce more fusion power.

Creating the ellipse requires a spatially varying PF field. Coil locations and
currents are selected so that the combined plasma and external fields support a tall
boundary. Shape is therefore an additional task for the same PF system that holds
the plasma's radial position. Elongation also changes its response to displacement.

\designconsequence{Elongation increases volume at fixed $R_0$ and $a$. Its benefit must be assessed together with the response to vertical displacement.}

\section*{14. A taller plasma needs position feedback}
To expose the stability question, represent a small vertical displacement by $z$.
Expand its force about equilibrium:
\begin{equation}
 F_Z(z)=F_Z(0)+K_z z+\cdots,\qquad
 K_z=\left.\frac{\partial F_Z}{\partial z}\right|_0.
 \label{eq:vertical-linearization}
\end{equation}
Since $F_Z(0)=0$, a one-coordinate inertial model gives
\begin{equation}
 M\ddot z=K_z z.
 \label{eq:vertical-mode}
\end{equation}
For $K_z<0$ a displacement produces a restoring force. For $K_z>0$ the growing
solution is $z\propto e^{\sqrt{K_z/M}t}$. This model explains the sign of the
feedback needed. \samplescope{A real vertical mode also couples to induced currents in nearby conducting structures and to the coil circuits; its growth rate requires those dynamics.}

Elongation generally increases the difficulty of vertical stabilization.\footnote{
J.~P.~Freidberg, A.~Cerfon and J.~P.~Lee, ``Tokamak elongation---how much is too
much? Part 1. Theory,'' \emph{Journal of Plasma Physics} \textbf{81}, 515810607
(2015), \href{https://arxiv.org/abs/1508.06658}{open manuscript}.}
Magnetic sensors measure signals from which plasma position is estimated. A controller
compares that position with the requested one and commands power supplies to change
control-coil currents. The resulting field must oppose the displacement quickly enough
to keep the plasma clear of the vessel. Conducting structures can slow the motion and
give the active system time to respond.

\designconsequence{An unstable vertical displacement requires active correction. Sensors, a controller and powered coils must close the restoring feedback loop.}

\section*{15. Shape also changes the current allowance}
The safety-factor relation from §7 can be generalized to the elongated geometry.
The reduced reactor model of Freidberg, Mangiarotti and Minervini adopts
\begin{equation}
 q_* =\frac{2\pi a^2B_0}{\mu_0R_0I_p}\frac{1+\kappa^2}{2}.
 \label{eq:qstar}
\end{equation}
The first factor is the circular edge estimate. The second accounts approximately
for elongation in this large-aspect-ratio description. We take this shaped relation
from the model, which uses it as a global measure of field-line pitch.

A \emph{kink} is a collective displacement that bends the current-carrying plasma.
The model represents its global current limit by requiring $q_*>q_K$, with an adopted
threshold $q_K\simeq2$. Substitution gives
\begin{equation}
 I_p<\frac{2\pi a^2B_0}{\mu_0R_0q_K}\frac{1+\kappa^2}{2}.
 \label{eq:ip-kink-limit}
\end{equation}
The shaped estimate and threshold are the closures used in Eqs.~(30) and (39) of the
2015 reference. \samplemodel{Profiles, conducting-wall response and operating regime affect the physical stability boundary. The current ceiling here uses the stated large-aspect-ratio closure and $q_K\simeq2$.}

Within that model, stronger toroidal field, greater minor radius and greater
elongation permit more current. Increasing $R_0$ while holding the other quantities
fixed reduces the allowance. This matters because the current that creates the
poloidal confinement field also enters stability limits. Elongation offers both more
volume and a larger current allowance, while demanding stronger position control.
These consequences must be considered together.

\designconsequence{Elongation raises the current allowance in the adopted model. The chosen shape must also remain within the vertical-control capability.}

\section*{16. The current profile determines the interior pitch}
Total current is an area integral. In the circular approximation,
\begin{equation}
 I_{\rm enc}(r)=2\pi\int_0^r j_\varphi(r')r'\,dr',\qquad
 q(r)\simeq\frac{2\pi r^2B_0}{\mu_0R_0I_{\rm enc}(r)}.
 \label{eq:qprofile}
\end{equation}
Two plasmas with the same $I_p=I_{\rm enc}(a)$ can therefore have different interior
$q(r)$ if their current density is distributed differently. A centrally concentrated
current places more enclosed current inside a small radius and lowers $q$ there
relative to a broader distribution with the same total current.

For example, a uniform $j_\varphi=j_0$ gives $I_{\rm enc}=\pi j_0r^2$ and a constant
$q\simeq2B_0/(\mu_0R_0j_0)$ in this approximation. A varying profile changes that
pitch from surface to surface. The radial variation of pitch is called
\emph{magnetic shear}. Both pitch and shear influence how disturbances couple across
magnetic surfaces. Rational surfaces, where $q=m/n$, allow perturbations with matching
periodicity to remain aligned with the field.

The transformer and auxiliary current-drive systems influence where current flows,
as well as how much flows. Current control must account for $j_\varphi(r)$ and
$q(r)$ alongside $I_p$.

\designconsequence{Total current alone does not fix $q(r)$. Current control must account for the distribution $j_\varphi(r)$ as well as $I_p$.}

\section*{17. Why field and volume dominate a first power estimate}
For an equal D--T mixture with total fuel-ion density $n$, $n_D=n_T=n/2$. In a uniform
plasma the reaction rate per unit volume is
$n_Dn_T\langle\sigma v\rangle=n^2\langle\sigma v\rangle/4$.
The \emph{reactivity} $\langle\sigma v\rangle$ averages the cross-section times
relative speed over the ion distributions. At fixed temperature and fuel mixture,
\begin{equation}
 P_{\rm fus}=\frac{n^2}{4}\langle\sigma v\rangle QV\propto n^2V.
 \label{eq:pfus-density}
\end{equation}
Using $p=2nT$ and $p=\beta B_0^2/(2\mu_0)$ gives
$n=\beta B_0^2/(4\mu_0T)$, and hence
\begin{equation}
 P_{\rm fus}\propto\beta^2B_0^4V
 \propto\beta^2B_0^4R_0a^2\kappa.
 \label{eq:pfus-scaling}
\end{equation}
\samplescope{This scaling holds temperature, composition and profile factors fixed, and uses $B_0$ as the reference magnetic field.} For example, a 10\% field increase gives a
factor $1.1^4\simeq1.46$ in fusion power if beta and volume can be maintained.
The fourth power explains the attraction of high field. Maintaining those other
quantities requires compatible confinement, current and stability; producing the
field also imposes loads on the magnet. The scaling identifies the incentive that
later engineering calculations must price.

\designconsequence{The conditional $\beta^2B_0^4V$ scaling rewards field, pressure and volume. These quantities must be achievable together in one operating state.}

\section*{19. Triangularity adds a D-shaped boundary}
Elongation specifies height relative to width. A second parameter measures the inward
shift of the upper and lower tips of the cross-section. A useful boundary family is
\begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
 R(\theta)&=R_0+a\cos(\theta+x\sin\theta),\qquad x=\arcsin\delta,\\
 Z(\theta)&=\kappa a\sin\theta.
 \end{aligned}
 \label{eq:miller-boundary}
\end{equation}
At the top, $\theta=\pi/2$, so $R_{\rm top}=R_0-a\sin x=R_0-\delta a$. Thus
\begin{equation}
 \delta=\frac{R_0-R_{\rm top}}a
 \label{eq:triangularity}
\end{equation}
is the \emph{triangularity}. Positive $\delta$ pulls the top and bottom inward,
producing a D-like cross-section. At $\delta=0$ the boundary is the ellipse of §13.
\samplemodel{The Miller parameterization specifies a boundary family. The small-$\delta$ harmonic expansion below retains terms through first order.}

For small $\delta$, expand $\cos(\theta+x\sin\theta)$ and use
$\sin^2\theta=(1-\cos2\theta)/2$:
\begin{equation}
 R(\theta)=R_0-\frac{a\delta}{2}+a\cos\theta+
 \frac{a\delta}{2}\cos2\theta+O(a\delta^2).
 \label{eq:triangular-harmonic}
\end{equation}
The added second angular harmonic and centring shift describe a shape change
independent of elongation. The PF system must supply another adjustable spatial
pattern to control it.

\designconsequence{Triangularity adds an independent boundary parameter. The PF system needs enough adjustable field patterns to control it alongside position and elongation.}

\section*{20. Coil placement controls the available shape patterns}
In a current-free vacuum region away from the coils,
$\nabla\times\mathbf B=0$ and $\nabla\cdot\mathbf B=0$. Locally we can write
$\mathbf B=-\nabla\Phi_m$, so the magnetic scalar potential satisfies
$\nabla^2\Phi_m=0$. A two-dimensional local example organizes the first patterns as
\[
 \Phi_1=C_1r\sin\theta,\qquad
 \Phi_2=C_2r^2\sin2\theta,\qquad
 \Phi_3=C_3r^3\sin3\theta.
\]
Each is harmonic. With local Cartesian coordinates $x=r\cos\theta$, $z=r\sin\theta$,
the first two become $C_1z$ and $2C_2xz$. Their fields are, respectively, uniform and
linear in position. The third potential, $C_3(3x^2z-z^3)$, gives a quadratic field.

A uniform vertical field changes the radial force on the plasma. The linear,
\emph{quadrupole} pattern contributes to elliptical shaping. The next,
\emph{hexapole} pattern supplies a further shaping degree of freedom useful for
triangularity. These names describe successive spatial patterns of the field. The
actual response of the boundary follows from force balance in toroidal geometry.



Each coil influences several patterns. Its position fixes that influence, while its
power supply sets the current. Independent control of position and shape therefore
requires enough independently adjustable coil circuits, with suitable placement and
current range. More detailed shaping makes this an increasingly coupled control problem.

\section*{21. Equilibrium joins pressure to magnetic surfaces}
The static fluid balance derived in §8 is
$\nabla p=\mathbf j\times\mathbf B$. Its projection along the field gave
$\mathbf B\cdot\nabla p=0$. In the nested-surface equilibrium considered here,
we therefore write $p=p(\psi)$: one pressure value for each flux surface.
The pressure profile specifies how that value changes from the hot interior to the edge.

This is the point where particle confinement becomes a collective design problem.
Pressure, plasma current and the field from the coils must satisfy force balance at
every point. \samplemodel{We retain the static, axisymmetric, isotropic-pressure model introduced in §8. Substantial flow or pressure anisotropy requires a generalized equilibrium.}
Each surface is labelled by its poloidal flux $\psi$, so $p=p(\psi)$ gives
both a pressure value and, through $dp/d\psi$, the pressure change from one surface
to the next. The spatial gradient is $\nabla p=(dp/d\psi)\nabla\psi$: its direction
is normal to the surface and its magnitude depends on the spacing of the contours.
This links a profile specified as a function of flux to the physical force density
that the magnetic field must balance. Within the stated model, the geometry can
therefore be reduced to a scalar differential equation.

\designconsequence{Force balance makes pressure constant on connected magnetic surfaces. Describing those surfaces requires a common flux coordinate.}

\section*{22. Two scalar functions describe the field}
Combine the poloidal flux representation of §10 with a toroidal component written as
$B_\varphi=F(R,Z)/R$:
\begin{equation}
 \mathbf B=\frac1R\nabla\psi\times\hat{\boldsymbol\varphi}
 +\frac FR\hat{\boldsymbol\varphi}.
 \label{eq:axisymmetric-B}
\end{equation}
Axisymmetry and $\nabla\cdot\mathbf B=0$ permit this representation away from the
coordinate axis. The contours of $\psi$ locate the magnetic surfaces, and the function
$F$ sets the toroidal field strength. The factor $1/R$ retains the toroidal geometry.

We have already established $p=p(\psi)$. The toroidal component of force balance
will now establish $F=F(\psi)$ as well. The two functions carry different information. Derivatives of $\psi(R,Z)$
give $B_R$ and $B_Z$, so closely spaced contours mean a stronger poloidal field
at the same $R$. Its contour shapes locate the magnetic surfaces and boundary.
Meanwhile $F=RB_\varphi$ fixes the toroidal field; it has units of tesla-metres,
whereas $\psi$ has flux units. In a current-free toroidal-field region $F$ is constant,
recovering $B_\varphi\propto1/R$. In the plasma, $F(\psi)$ can vary across surfaces
and contributes to the current and force balance.

Specifying $p(\psi)$ and $F(\psi)$ thus supplies two surface profiles. It still
leaves $\psi(R,Z)$ to be found: the map from those surface labels to real space.
The Grad--Shafranov equation determines that map with the imposed boundary or
external-coil conditions. Its solution simultaneously supplies the poloidal field,
flux-surface geometry and toroidal current density.

\samplepair{The flux function specifies the surfaces and poloidal field. The next step is to solve for it using pressure and toroidal-field profiles.}{\includegraphics[width=.93\linewidth]{figures/final/figS_reading_psi.pdf}}{Flux contours and their normal identify the poloidal field geometry.}

\section*{23. Deriving the Grad--Shafranov equation}
Insert Eq.~\eqref{eq:axisymmetric-B} into
$\mu_0\mathbf j=\nabla\times\mathbf B$. The toroidal current density comes from
the curl of the poloidal field:
\begin{equation}
 j_\varphi=-\frac1{\mu_0R}\Delta^*\psi,\qquad
 \Delta^*\psi\equiv R\frac{\partial}{\partial R}
 \left(\frac1R\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial R}\right)
 +\frac{\partial^2\psi}{\partial Z^2}.
 \label{eq:gradstar}
\end{equation}
To check the sign, use
$(\nabla\times\mathbf B)_\varphi=\partial_ZB_R-\partial_RB_Z$ and the components
in Eq.~\eqref{eq:psidef}. The remaining components give
\[
 \mathbf j_p=\frac1{\mu_0R}\nabla F\times\hat{\boldsymbol\varphi}.
\]
Since pressure is axisymmetric, the toroidal component of $\mathbf j\times\mathbf B$
must vanish. Its value is proportional to $\mathbf B_p\cdot\nabla F$, so
$F$ is constant along the poloidal field. On the nested surfaces we can write
$F=F(\psi)$ and hence $\mathbf j_p=F'(\psi)\mathbf B_p/\mu_0$.

Only two cross products remain: $j_\varphi\hat{\boldsymbol\varphi}\times\mathbf B_p$
and $\mathbf j_p\times B_\varphi\hat{\boldsymbol\varphi}$. Using
$\hat{\boldsymbol\varphi}\times\mathbf B_p=\nabla\psi/R$, they give
\[
 \mathbf j\times\mathbf B
 =-\frac{\Delta^*\psi+FF'}{\mu_0R^2}\nabla\psi,
 \qquad \nabla p=p'\nabla\psi.
\]
Equating coefficients normal to a regular flux surface yields
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{\Delta^*\psi=-\mu_0R^2\frac{dp}{d\psi}-F\frac{dF}{d\psi}.}
 \label{eq:grad-shafranov}
\end{equation}
This is the \emph{Grad--Shafranov equation}. The pressure gradient and toroidal-field
profile supply its source terms; the solution supplies the flux surfaces.

\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\samplescope{At a smooth magnetic axis or null, the equation is interpreted through the regular solution. The intermediate cancellation of $\nabla\psi$ applies where that gradient is nonzero.}
\end{minipage}

The derivation reveals why geometry alone is insufficient to specify a tokamak.
Changing pressure or current changes the differential equation that its magnetic
surfaces must satisfy. Coil settings then have to support the resulting equilibrium.


\section*{24. From an equilibrium equation to an operating machine}
There are two useful ways to pose the calculation. In a~\emph{fixed-boundary} calculation we prescribe the plasma boundary and the profiles $p(\psi)$ and $F(\psi)$,
then solve for the interior flux surfaces and current. In a~\emph{free-boundary} calculation we include the external-coil fields and surrounding vacuum; the plasma
boundary is part of the solution. A desired shape can thus be tested first, then
translated into requirements on coil locations and currents. The analytic equilibria
cited in §10 illustrate how profiles and boundary geometry enter this problem.

The magnetic axis generally shifts outward relative to the geometric centre of the
boundary. This \mbox{\emph{Shafranov shift}} reflects the pressure and current contributions
to toroidal force balance. Its size depends on the profiles and geometry. When those
profiles change during heating or a current ramp, the equilibrium changes, and the
PF currents needed to maintain the requested boundary change with it.

A D-shaped plasma and a D-shaped TF coil have separate design purposes. Triangularity
specifies a plasma boundary chosen through equilibrium and stability considerations.
A D-like TF-coil shape can instead be chosen to carry magnetic loads mainly in tension
and reduce conductor bending. The engineering design must accommodate both the
required magnetic surfaces and the forces on the conductors that produce their field.

We have reached a working description of machine formation. Hot charged particles
require a vacuum environment and magnetic guidance; closing the field introduces
curvature drifts; plasma current supplies the poloidal twist; pressure demands
external equilibrium fields; transformer limits demand auxiliary heating and current
drive; useful shaping demands feedback. The Grad--Shafranov equation joins these
choices into a calculable equilibrium. The next block asks what fuel state and
confinement this machine must sustain to produce useful fusion power.

\designconsequence{Pressure and current evolution change the equilibrium. Each operating state needs compatible profiles, a magnetic boundary and PF-coil settings.}

\section*{25. From individual collisions to a fusion-power density}

Consider a small volume $dV$ containing $n_DdV$ deuterons and $n_TdV$ tritons.
Choose one deuteron. During a short time $dt$, tritons with relative speed $v$
sweep through the effective collision tube of area $\sigma(v)$ and length $vdt$.
The expected number of its reactions is
\[
 n_T\,\sigma(v)\,v\,dt.
\]
Particles in a thermal plasma do not all have the same relative speed. Average this
expression over the normalized relative-speed distribution and multiply by the
number $n_DdV$ of deuterons. Dividing by $dVdt$ gives the reaction rate per unit
volume:
\begin{equation}
 {\cal R}_{DT}=n_Dn_T\langle\sigma v\rangle,\qquad
 \langle\sigma v\rangle=\int_0^\infty \sigma(v)v\,f_{\rm rel}(v)\,dv .
 \label{eq:reaction-rate}
\end{equation}
The measured cross-section $\sigma(v)$ supplies the nuclear reaction probability;
$f_{\rm rel}(v)$ supplies the thermal statistics.

If each reaction releases total energy $E_f=17.6\,\mathrm{MeV}$, then
\begin{equation}
 p_{\rm fus}=E_f{\cal R}_{DT}
 =E_f n_Dn_T\langle\sigma v\rangle,\qquad
 P_{\rm fus}=p_{\rm fus}V.
 \label{eq:pfus-exact}
\end{equation}
This gives the factors behind the scaling in §17: density, volume and the
temperature-dependent reactivity jointly determine fusion output.

\samplemodel{Equation~\eqref{eq:reaction-rate} assumes independent binary collisions and
a thermal distribution. At reactor density, D--T fusion remains dilute enough for that
description; collective plasma physics controls confinement, not the short-range
nuclear reaction once two ions meet.}

\designconsequence{Fusion output depends on the product $n_Dn_T$. At fixed fuel density, the mixture must maximize that product.}

\section*{26. Why the fuel mixture is nearly fifty--fifty}

Let the total fuel-ion density be fixed:
\[
 n_i=n_D+n_T.
\]
Write $n_D=xn_i$. Then $n_T=(1-x)n_i$, and the density product in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:pfus-exact} is
\begin{equation}
 n_Dn_T=x(1-x)n_i^2.
 \label{eq:mixture-product}
\end{equation}
Complete the square:
\[
 x(1-x)=x-x^2=\frac14-\left(x-\frac12\right)^2.
\]
The squared term is nonnegative, so the largest possible value is $1/4$, achieved
at $x=1/2$. Thus
\begin{equation}
 n_D=n_T=\frac{n_i}{2}
 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
 p_{\rm fus}=\frac14n_i^2\langle\sigma v\rangle E_f.
 \label{eq:pfus-5050}
\end{equation}
Equal fuel fractions maximize the reaction rate at fixed total fuel-ion density and temperature.

The pressure used in magnetic confinement also includes electrons. A quasi-neutral
50--50 D--T mixture has electron density $n_e=n_i$. We write the primary fuel
density as $n\equiv n_i$. If the ions and electrons share
a temperature $T$ measured in energy units, their ideal-gas pressures add:
\begin{equation}
 p=(n_i+n_e)T=2n_iT.
 \label{eq:plasma-pressure}
\end{equation}
Combining Eqs.~\eqref{eq:pfus-5050} and \eqref{eq:plasma-pressure} explains the
scaling chain on §17, but with its factors visible: at fixed $T$,
$n_i=p/(2T)$ and hence $p_{\rm fus}\propto p^2$.

\designconsequence{The density needed for fusion also creates pressure. The same fuel state must satisfy the magnetic-pressure and density constraints.}

\section*{27. Where the fusion energy goes decides what the machine must add}

The D--T products are an alpha particle and a neutron. In the center-of-mass frame
they leave with equal and opposite momentum, so their momentum magnitudes are equal:
$m_\alpha v_\alpha=m_nv_n$. Their kinetic energies are
$E_\alpha=p^2/(2m_\alpha)$ and $E_n=p^2/(2m_n)$. Therefore
\[
 \frac{E_n}{E_\alpha}=\frac{m_\alpha}{m_n}\simeq4,\qquad
 E_\alpha=\frac15E_f\simeq3.5\,\mathrm{MeV},\quad
 E_n=\frac45E_f\simeq14.1\,\mathrm{MeV}.
\]
The charged alpha particle can remain confined long enough to slow down by
collisions and heat the plasma. The neutron has no electric charge, so magnetic
fields do not bend it; it escapes the plasma and deposits its energy only after
meeting material outside the vacuum region.

With complete alpha-energy deposition, $P_\alpha=(E_\alpha/E_f)P_{\rm fus}\simeq P_{\rm fus}/5$.
Neutron power is $P_n\simeq4P_{\rm fus}/5$. The first term enters the plasma
self-heating balance; the second must be captured outside the confined plasma.
\samplemodel{The power balances below assume thermal fuel, common ion and electron
temperature, and complete alpha-energy deposition. Alpha escape reduces the deposited fraction.}

\designconsequence{Alpha energy can sustain the plasma; neutron energy must be collected in surrounding material. The two fractions enter different power balances.}

\section*{28. Stored energy and the meaning of confinement time}

Before asking whether alpha heating is enough, we need a careful measure of loss.
For an ideal gas with three translational degrees of freedom, the internal energy
density is $3nT/2$. In the equal-temperature D--T plasma there are $n_i$ ions and
$n_e=n_i$ electrons, so the total thermal energy is
\begin{equation}
 W=\left(\frac32n_iT+\frac32n_eT\right)V
  =3n_iTV.
 \label{eq:stored-energy}
\end{equation}
The energy-confinement time is defined, rather than guessed, by
\begin{equation}
 \tau_E\equiv\frac{W}{P_{\rm loss}},
 \qquad P_{\rm loss}=\frac{W}{\tau_E}.
 \label{eq:taue-def}
\end{equation}
It answers a plain question: if all heating stopped and the loss rate stayed at its
instantaneous value, how long would the present thermal energy take to leave?

Here $P_{\rm loss}$ includes transport and radiation, each counted once. The
instantaneous ratio $W/P_{\rm loss}$ remains useful even when the energy decay
is not exponential.

Using Eq.~\eqref{eq:plasma-pressure}, Eq.~\eqref{eq:stored-energy} can also be
written $W=(3/2)pV$. Thus a high-pressure plasma stores more energy at a given
volume, but it also demands proportionally more heating power if $\tau_E$ is fixed.

\designconsequence{The loss power is $W/\tau_E$. Ignition requires alpha heating to cover it at the proposed density and temperature.}

\section*{29. The Lawson condition follows from one inequality}

Ignition means that alpha heating alone can replace the plasma losses:
\begin{equation}
 P_\alpha\ge P_{\rm loss}.
 \label{eq:ignition-start}
\end{equation}
Use the equal-mixture fusion result, Eq.~\eqref{eq:pfus-5050}, but retain only the
alpha energy $E_\alpha$:
\[
 P_\alpha=\frac14n_i^2\langle\sigma v\rangle E_\alpha V.
\]
Use Eq.~\eqref{eq:stored-energy} and Eq.~\eqref{eq:taue-def} for the loss:
\[
 P_{\rm loss}=\frac{3n_iTV}{\tau_E}.
\]
Substitution into Eq.~\eqref{eq:ignition-start} gives
\[
 \frac14n_i^2\langle\sigma v\rangle E_\alpha V
 \ge \frac{3n_iTV}{\tau_E}.
\]
Cancel the positive common factor $n_iV$, then multiply by $4\tau_E$:
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{n_i\tau_E\ge
 \frac{12T}{\langle\sigma v\rangle E_\alpha}.}
 \label{eq:lawson}
\end{equation}
Every factor has a visible origin: $1/4$ from the equal fuel mix, $3$ from ions plus
electrons and three translational degrees of freedom, and $E_\alpha$ rather than
$E_f$ because neutrons do not heat the confined plasma.

Multiplying Eq.~\eqref{eq:lawson} by $T$ gives the triple product $n_iT\tau_E$.
Its threshold depends on the chosen temperature and D--T reactivity.

\designconsequence{Ignition requires a compatible combination of density, temperature and confinement. External heating supplies any remaining power deficit.}

\section*{30. Gain is a ratio, not a synonym for ignition}

A plasma can make useful fusion power without being ignited if external heating
supplies the missing energy. Define the plasma gain
\begin{equation}
 Q_{\rm plasma}\equiv\frac{P_{\rm fus}}{P_{\rm aux}},
 \label{eq:qplasma}
\end{equation}
where $P_{\rm aux}$ is the heating delivered to the plasma by neutral beams, radio
frequency systems, or other actuators. In steady state, a stripped-down energy
balance is
\begin{equation}
 P_\alpha+P_{\rm aux}=P_{\rm loss}.
 \label{eq:power-balance}
\end{equation}
As $P_{\rm aux}$ tends to zero while the equality remains possible, $Q_{\rm plasma}$
tends to infinity: that limiting case is ignition. At finite $Q_{\rm plasma}$, the
plant must supply auxiliary power and also pay the inefficiency of producing and
delivering it.

Net electric power additionally depends on neutron-energy capture, thermal
conversion and recirculating loads such as refrigeration, pumps and controls.
$Q_{\rm plasma}$ measures the plasma contribution to that wider power balance.

\designconsequence{Finite plasma gain requires auxiliary power. Its required level depends on transport through $\tau_E$.}

\section*{31. Why transport is the unresolved clock}

A particle in a uniform magnetic field circles with a characteristic gyroradius
$\rho$. A collision changes the direction of its velocity; the center of the next
gyro-orbit is displaced by an amount of order $\rho$. If collisions occur at rate
$\nu$, then in time $t$ there are approximately $N=\nu t$ independent steps.
Random-walk addition gives
\[
 \langle\Delta r^2\rangle\sim N\rho^2=\nu t\rho^2.
\]
By the definition $\langle\Delta r^2\rangle\sim2Dt$ in one radial direction, the
classical cross-field diffusion estimate is
\begin{equation}
 D_{\rm cl}\sim\frac{\rho^2\nu}{2}.
 \label{eq:classical-diffusion}
\end{equation}
A container of minor radius $a$ would then have a diffusion time of order
\begin{equation}
 \tau_{\rm diff}\sim\frac{a^2}{D_{\rm cl}}.
 \label{eq:diffusion-time}
\end{equation}
This is a transparent reason strong field helps: $\rho\propto1/B$, so the classical
diffusion coefficient falls roughly as $1/B^2$ if the collision rate is held fixed.

Measured energy transport often exceeds this classical estimate. Fluctuations
and turbulence correlate motion across flux surfaces. The random walk supplies a
baseline; reactor prediction uses a confinement model calibrated to experiments.

\samplefigure{\includegraphics[width=.95\linewidth]{figures/final/figS_random_walk.pdf}}{Independent collisional steps provide a classical transport scale; correlated fluctuations add further transport.\label{fig:figS_random_walk}}

\designconsequence{Cross-field transport sets the loss time. Reactor calculations therefore need a confinement model with a stated applicability range.}

\section*{32. Confinement is calibrated to experiments}

For reactor studies, the working confinement time is often represented as
\begin{equation}
 \tau_E=H\,\tau_{E,\rm ref}(I_p,B_0,n,P_{\rm loss},R_0,a,\kappa,\ldots).
 \label{eq:Hfactor}
\end{equation}
Here $\tau_{E,\rm ref}$ is a regression fitted to a specified database of
experiments, and $H$ is the ratio of the observed confinement to that reference.
Its use requires the database, operating regime, units, heating conditions and
shape range of the selected regression. Treat the regression as data with a stated
applicability range.

The distinction between a derived equation and a fit matters especially here.
Equation~\eqref{eq:classical-diffusion} followed from a stated random-walk model.
Equation~\eqref{eq:Hfactor} is a bookkeeping identity once a reference scaling is
chosen; its predictive content lives in the data and the applicability range of
$\tau_{E,\rm ref}$.

A practical design calculation must therefore report at least three things: the
selected reference law, the assumed $H$, and a sensitivity range. An assumed $H$ above comparable experimental performance identifies the
confinement improvement the design requires.

\samplescope{Confinement is represented here by an empirical reference law and multiplier $H$.
The selected law, units and applicability range accompany the numerical reconstruction.}

\designconsequence{Confinement closes the energy balance alongside geometry and pressure. The assumed reference law and $H$ must be tested together.}

\section*{33. Pressure cannot rise freely: the origin of a beta limit}

The local pressure-versus-line-bending balance gives a scaling estimate.
Practical stability thresholds require a specified equilibrium and stability model.

The beta in §8 compares plasma pressure with magnetic pressure:
\[
 \beta=\frac{p}{B_0^2/(2\mu_0)}.
\]
Why should there be an upper limit? Imagine a bulge of a pressure-carrying flux tube.
The pressure force trying to enlarge it scales as $p$ times its cross-sectional area.
Bending the field lines that surround it costs magnetic energy; the restoring scale is
set by $B_\theta^2/\mu_0$, where $B_\theta$ is the poloidal field that ties the
plasma current to the geometry.

Ampere's-law scale at the edge is
\[
 B_\theta\sim\frac{\mu_0I_p}{2\pi a}.
\]
Equating the pressure and field-bending scales gives
\[
 p_{\max}\sim\frac{B_\theta^2}{\mu_0}
 \sim\frac{\mu_0I_p^2}{4\pi^2a^2}.
\]
Divide by $B_0^2/(2\mu_0)$ to obtain the dimensionless structural form
\begin{equation}
 \beta_{\max}\propto\left(\frac{I_p}{aB_0}\right)^2
 \quad\hbox{in this crude local balance.}
 \label{eq:beta-scale-crude}
\end{equation}
The balance identifies pressure drive and magnetic restoring force. A global
ideal-MHD calculation includes geometry and profiles when determining a stability boundary.

\samplefigure{\includegraphics[width=.95\linewidth]{figures/final/figS_pressure_vs_bending.pdf}}{Pressure drives a bulge while magnetic line bending supplies a restoring force. The local balance gives a scaling estimate.\label{fig:figS_pressure_vs_bending}}

\designconsequence{Pressure support depends on current, field and shape. A practical stability constraint must express that coupling.}

\section*{34. The normalized beta used in reactor design}

Detailed ideal-MHD calculations and experiments are commonly summarized by a
normalized beta. With $\beta$ written as a fraction rather than a percentage, define
\begin{equation}
 \beta_N\equiv
 \frac{\beta\,a(\mathrm m)\,B_0(\mathrm T)}{I_p(\mathrm{MA})}.
 \label{eq:betaN}
\end{equation}
This numerical convention uses $a$ in metres, $B_0$ in tesla and $I_p$ in MA.
Using beta in percent instead would give a normalized-beta value 100 times larger. It is useful because comparable tokamak equilibria show an approximate
operating boundary of the form
\begin{equation}
 \beta\lesssim\beta_{N,\max}
 \frac{I_p(\mathrm{MA})}{a(\mathrm m)B_0(\mathrm T)}.
 \label{eq:troyon-form}
\end{equation}
The coefficient $\beta_{N,\max}$ is empirical/model-dependent; it must be imported
from a specified stability study or design paper.

Equation~\eqref{eq:troyon-form} makes the current--pressure coupling explicit:
larger allowable current raises allowable pressure, while a larger $aB_0$ at fixed
current lowers this particular normalized beta. Combining it with the current ceiling
on §15 makes the coupling visible: the kink limit restricts $I_p$, which then
restricts the pressure that can be claimed for fusion power.

\designconsequence{The pressure allowance depends on the current permitted by kink stability. Both constraints must hold at the same operating point.}

\section*{35. Density has a separate experimental limit}

It would be tempting to use Eq.~\eqref{eq:plasma-pressure} and conclude that a chosen
pressure can always be obtained by trading density against temperature. Tokamak
operation does not permit an unlimited trade. A widely used experimental reference is
the Greenwald density
\begin{equation}
 n_G=
 \frac{I_p(\mathrm{MA})}{\pi a^2(\mathrm m^2)}
 \times10^{20}\ \mathrm{m^{-3}}.
 \label{eq:greenwald}
\end{equation}
Operational scenarios usually report the fraction $f_G=n_e/n_G$, rather than merely
a raw density.

\samplescope{The Greenwald relation is an empirical operating reference. The chosen
scenario must state the density averaging convention and allowable fraction $f_G$;
the numerical comparison uses a line-averaged electron density.}

The formula still carries useful transparent geometry. At fixed current, a smaller
cross-section raises the reference density as $1/a^2$. At fixed $a$, more current
raises it. But that same current is limited by $q_*$ and the same density contributes
to pressure and radiation. The fraction $f_G$ records the resulting density demand relative to the reference.

\designconsequence{Density has its own operating constraint. Increasing it must remain compatible with pressure, current and confinement.}

\section*{36. Toroidal geometry traps some particles}

The toroidal field is stronger on the inboard side because approximately
$B_\varphi\propto1/R$. A particle moving into stronger field converts parallel
motion into perpendicular motion while conserving, to lowest adiabatic order,
\[
 \mu=\frac{mv_\perp^2}{2B}
 \quad\hbox{and}\quad
 \frac12m(v_\parallel^2+v_\perp^2).
\]
Let the particle start where the field is $B_{\min}$ and let $\alpha$ be its pitch
angle there, so $v_\perp=v\sin\alpha$. At the turning point $v_\parallel=0$.
Conservation of $\mu$ gives
\[
 \frac{v^2\sin^2\alpha}{B_{\min}}=\frac{v^2}{B_{\max}},
 \qquad
 \sin^2\alpha=\frac{B_{\min}}{B_{\max}}.
\]
Particles with larger perpendicular fraction reach this condition before the high-field
side and bounce back. For isotropically distributed velocity directions evaluated at $B_{\min}$, the local
trapped fraction is therefore
\begin{equation}
 f_t=\sqrt{1-\frac{B_{\min}}{B_{\max}}}.
 \label{eq:trapped-fraction}
\end{equation}
The same $1/R$ field that closes the magnetic corridor therefore creates trapped
orbits. Neoclassical transport combines these toroidal guiding-centre orbits with
Coulomb collisions; pressure gradients also produce a self-generated current.

\samplefigure{\includegraphics[width=.95\linewidth]{figures/final/figS_trapped_orbit.pdf}}{Passing and trapped guiding-centre orbits. Turning points follow from conservation of magnetic moment and energy; orbit widths are schematic.\label{fig:figS_trapped_orbit}}

\designconsequence{Toroidal field variation creates trapped particles. Their response to a pressure gradient introduces a source of plasma current.}

\section*{37. A pressure gradient can generate some of the required current}

A trapped particle does not sample the two sides of the torus symmetrically. Its
guiding-center drifts and collisions depend on energy and pitch angle. When pressure
falls outward, there are more energetic particles on the inner side of a local radial
interval than on the outer side. The resulting imbalance in trapped-particle motion is
communicated by collisions to passing electrons. The outcome is a net toroidal current
called the \emph{bootstrap current}.

The direction and detailed coefficient require kinetic theory, because they depend on
collisions, geometry, temperature gradients, and the current profile. What can be
stated before importing such a coefficient is the structure:
\begin{equation}
 j_{\rm bs}\propto-\frac{dp}{dr}
 \quad\hbox{and}\quad
 I_{\rm bs}=\int j_{\rm bs}\,dA.
 \label{eq:bootstrap-structure}
\end{equation}
No pressure gradient means no bootstrap drive in this simplified statement. A steeper
gradient can produce more self-driven current, but it also moves the plasma closer to
pressure-driven stability limits. Bootstrap current couples the pressure profile to the current needed for magnetic geometry.

Define the bootstrap fraction
\begin{equation}
 f_{\rm bs}\equiv\frac{I_{\rm bs}}{I_p}.
 \label{eq:bootstrap-fraction}
\end{equation}
The fraction $f_{\rm bs}$ is the primary parameter $f_B$. A kinetic model supplies
its profile-dependent value, which sets the remaining transformer or external-drive demand.

\samplemodel{The proportionality describes the pressure-gradient drive in a reduced model.
A quantitative prediction requires geometry, collisionality, species gradients and profiles.}

\designconsequence{The pressure profile supplies part of the current. Its bootstrap contribution must remain compatible with stability and confinement.}

\section*{38. The full current ledger}

The toroidal plasma current can have several sources. Write the accounting identity
\begin{equation}
 I_p=I_{\rm ohm}+I_{\rm CD}+I_{\rm bs}.
 \label{eq:current-ledger}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:current-ledger} is an accounting identity; performance enters
through the three terms. The first term is transformer-induced ohmic current; it is available while the
central solenoid changes linked flux. The second is externally driven non-inductive
current, for example from waves or beams transferring directed momentum. The third
is the pressure-gradient-driven bootstrap current from §37.

For a stationary non-inductive state with negligible transformer contribution,
$I_{\rm ohm}\simeq0$, and Eq.~\eqref{eq:current-ledger} reduces to
\begin{equation}
 I_{\rm CD}=(1-f_{\rm bs})I_p.
 \label{eq:current-drive-need}
\end{equation}
The larger the bootstrap fraction, the smaller the externally driven current
requirement. But current drive consumes power and its efficiency depends on density,
temperature, magnetic field, launch geometry, and the actuator itself. The bootstrap fraction must come from a compatible profile model.

\designconsequence{The required current must be supplied by transformer action, bootstrap current and affordable external drive. Their balance depends on operating mode.}

\section*{39. Pulsed operation is also a legitimate design choice}

The central solenoid is a transformer primary. Faraday's law gives the loop voltage
around the torus:
\[
 \oint\mathbf E\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell=-\frac{d\Phi_{\rm link}}{dt}.
\]
For a torus of major radius $R_0$, a representative toroidal electric field satisfies
$2\pi R_0E_\varphi=-d\Phi_{\rm link}/dt$. The solenoid has a finite usable flux swing
$\Delta\Phi_{\rm link}$, so it can induce current only for a finite time. This is the
physical origin of the transformer clock introduced on §11.

There are two coherent responses. One is a pulsed plant: use transformer current
during a burn, then recharge the solenoid between pulses. The other is a long-pulse
or steady-state plant: replace the lost ohmic contribution with the current-drive and
bootstrap terms in Eq.~\eqref{eq:current-ledger}. Neither option is free. Pulsing
creates thermal cycling and dwell-time questions; non-inductive operation creates
actuator power and accessibility questions. The operating choice must account for both the plant duty cycle and actuator demand.

\samplefigure{\includegraphics[width=.95\linewidth]{figures/final/figS_close_the_ledger.pdf}}{Two current-supply strategies: transformer-supported burns with recharge intervals, or non-inductive current sustaining the burn. The illustrated current shares are schematic.\label{fig:figS_close_the_ledger}}

\designconsequence{Transformer flux limits the inductive burn. Extending it requires more usable flux or a larger non-inductive current contribution.}

\section*{40. The kink limit returns as a constraint on every current source}

The current ledger does not allow arbitrary total current. A helical disturbance can
be labelled by integers $(m,n)$ through a displacement pattern
$\xi\propto\cos(m\theta-n\varphi)$. Along an unperturbed field line, a poloidal turn
changes $\theta$ by $2\pi$ while the toroidal angle changes by $2\pi q$. The phase
change is therefore $2\pi(m-nq)$. When $q=m/n$, the disturbance remains in phase
after a circuit; field-line bending has the least geometric leverage to average it
away.

This is the accessible reason safety factor is a stability variable rather than only
a geometric ratio. The full ideal-MHD energy principle determines the precise
stability boundary. Reactor studies commonly use a specified global constraint
\begin{equation}
 q_*>q_K,
 \label{eq:kink-constraint-repeat}
\end{equation}
with $q_K$ supplied by the chosen stability model. Substituting the shaped
safety-factor formula, Eq.~\eqref{eq:qstar}, reproduces the current ceiling already
derived in Eq.~\eqref{eq:ip-kink-limit}. Every term in the current ledger must fit
under that same ceiling; bootstrap current helps meet a required $I_p$ but does not
abolish the stability constraint on $I_p$ itself.

\samplemodel{The resonance argument motivates the role of field-line pitch. The global
threshold $q_K$ is supplied separately by the selected stability model.}

\designconsequence{Kink stability constrains the total current, regardless of its source. Bootstrap current reduces external drive demand within that same ceiling.}

\section*{41. The operating constraints must overlap}

For an ignited candidate state, the coupled operating requirements include
\begin{align}
 q_*&>q_K &&\text{current and kink margin},\label{eq:wall-q}\\
 \beta&\lesssim\beta_{N,\max}\frac{I_p}{aB_0}
 &&\text{pressure and MHD margin},\label{eq:wall-beta}\\
 n_e&\lesssim n_G &&\text{density reference},\label{eq:wall-density}\\
 n_i\tau_E&\ge\frac{12T}{\langle\sigma v\rangle E_\alpha}
 &&\text{self-heating requirement},\label{eq:wall-lawson}\\
 I_p&=I_{\rm ohm}+I_{\rm CD}+I_{\rm bs}
 &&\text{current accounting}.\label{eq:wall-current}
\end{align}
The beta relation uses the metre--tesla--MA convention of §34.
The notation $\lesssim$ in the second and third lines is deliberate. Those limits
are modelled or empirical margins, not exact equalities. The last line is an
identity, but its terms depend on operating mode and profiles. The first and fourth
lines are inequalities derived from explicit physical balances, with a stability
threshold or reactivity curve still to be specified.

Increasing density might help Eq.~\eqref{eq:wall-lawson} but challenge
Eq.~\eqref{eq:wall-density}; increasing current can improve one normalized beta
bound but challenge Eq.~\eqref{eq:wall-q}; improving confinement changes needed
heating and current drive. The correct question is whether the inequalities overlap
with usable margin.

\designconsequence{An operating point must satisfy the coupled constraints with margin. The remaining geometric input is the radial space required around the plasma.}

\section*{42. From plasma requirements to the radial build}

The plasma-side roles of Freidberg's Table-1 parameters:~$a,R_0,\kappa,T,n,p,\tau_E,B_0,\beta,I,q_*,f_B$ have now been introduced.
Quantities such as $A,\delta,q(r),\beta_N,H,I_{\rm CD},Q_{\rm plasma}$ remain
supporting quantities, not independent primary knobs. The missing hardware
parameters are~$b$ and~$c$; they must be derived before numerical closure begins.
Neutron interactions, magnetic forces and current-carrying area determine their roles.

\designconsequence{Neutron capture and magnet forces require radial space. Deriving $b$ and $c$ completes the reactor geometry.}

\section*{43. A magnetic bottle does not catch the neutron}

The D--T reaction in Eq.~\eqref{eq:dt} has two products with very different fates.
The charged alpha particle remains magnetically guided and can transfer energy back to
the plasma. The neutron has no electric charge. Its Lorentz force is exactly zero:
\[
 \mathbf F_n=0\cdot(\mathbf E+\mathbf v_n\times\mathbf B)=0.
\]
It crosses the magnetic surfaces in a nearly straight line until it interacts
with surrounding material.

The neutron carries $14.1\,\mathrm{MeV}$, approximately four fifths of the fusion
energy. A power-producing machine must therefore do four jobs outside the plasma:
survive the first impact, slow and capture neutrons, breed replacement tritium, and
protect the magnets and vacuum boundary from the remaining radiation. These jobs
force a radial region between plasma and TF coil. We call its total inboard/outboard
thickness $b$.

\designconsequence{Neutron energy requires a surrounding nuclear region. Its thickness competes with the radius available to the inboard magnet.}

\samplescope{The following one-dimensional model relates thickness to interaction lengths.
Material-specific neutron transport, heat removal and tritium recovery determine the engineered build.}

\section*{44. Why the nuclear island has a thickness}

First consider the slowing-down part of the problem. In a homogeneous material with
target number density $N$ and an effective interaction cross section $\sigma_s$, the
mean distance between relevant collisions is
\begin{equation}
 \lambda_s=\frac{1}{N\sigma_s}.
 \label{eq:mean-free-path}
\end{equation}
This follows directly from probability. In a thin distance $dx$, a neutron has
collision probability $N\sigma_sdx=dx/\lambda_s$. Thus the chance of no collision
decreases exponentially with distance.

For a transparent first model, suppose each collision removes a fixed fraction $\xi$
of the neutron energy on average. Over many collisions, the average energy loss per
distance can be represented by
\begin{equation}
 \frac{dE}{dx}=-\frac{\xi E}{\lambda_s}.
 \label{eq:slowing-down}
\end{equation}
Separating variables gives
\[
 \frac{dE}{E}=-\frac{\xi\,dx}{\lambda_s}
 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
 E(x)=E_0\exp\!\left(-\frac{\xi x}{\lambda_s}\right).
\]
To reduce a $14.1\,\mathrm{MeV}$ neutron from $E_0$ to a chosen capture-friendly
energy $E_c$, the associated slowing-down distance is therefore
\begin{equation}
 b_{\rm slow}=\frac{\lambda_s}{\xi}\ln\!\left(\frac{E_0}{E_c}\right).
 \label{eq:bslow}
\end{equation}
The logarithm matters: a large energy ratio does not require a proportional thickness,
but it does require several interaction lengths. Equation~\eqref{eq:bslow} already
shows why a thin wall cannot be a reactor solution.

Now consider breeding. One useful reaction is
\[
 n+{}^6\mathrm{Li}\longrightarrow{}^4\mathrm{He}+{}^3\mathrm{H}
 +4.8\,\mathrm{MeV}.
\]
The emitted tritium replaces fuel consumed by D--T fusion. If slow neutrons are
absorbed with macroscopic probability per length $\Sigma_B=N_6\sigma_B$, their
surviving flux $\Gamma$ obeys
\[
 \frac{d\Gamma}{dx}=-\Sigma_B\Gamma,
 \qquad
 \Gamma(x)=\Gamma(0)e^{-x/\lambda_B},
 \qquad
 \lambda_B=\frac1{\Sigma_B}.
\]
Capturing a chosen fraction $f_{\rm cap}$ then requires
\begin{equation}
 b_{\rm breed}=-\lambda_B\ln(1-f_{\rm cap}).
 \label{eq:bbreed}
\end{equation}
Both $\lambda_s$ and $\lambda_B$ depend on material and neutron energy. Thus
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:bslow}--\eqref{eq:bbreed} retain those material and energy dependencies.

\section*{45. From microscopic collisions to the reactor dimension $b$}

The nuclear region performs several functions. At the level
needed for a first reactor layout, write
\begin{equation}
 b=b_{\rm FW}+b_{\rm mult}+b_{\rm slow}+b_{\rm breed}
   +b_{\rm shield}+b_{\rm vac}.
 \label{eq:blanket-build}
\end{equation}
Here $b_{\rm FW}$ is the first-wall allowance; $b_{\rm mult}$ represents any
neutron-multiplying region; $b_{\rm slow}$ and $b_{\rm breed}$ are motivated by the
preceding transport calculation; $b_{\rm shield}$ protects coils and surrounding
structures; and $b_{\rm vac}$ is the vacuum-boundary allowance. Some concepts combine
or reorder these functions. The terms in Eq.~\eqref{eq:blanket-build} are non-overlapping radial allowances;
combined functions are counted once.

The first wall also sees a power-density demand. In the large-aspect-ratio circular
approximation, a torus with major radius $R_0$ and minor radius $a$ has surface area
\[
 S_{\rm wall}\simeq (2\pi R_0)(2\pi a)=4\pi^2R_0a.
\]
If $P_n$ is the neutron power and $W_n$ is an allowed average neutron wall loading,
\begin{equation}
 \frac{P_n}{4\pi^2R_0a}\le W_n
 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
 R_0a\ge\frac{P_n}{4\pi^2W_n}.
 \label{eq:wall-loading-geometry}
\end{equation}
Elongation and detailed surface shape modify this estimate; they do not remove its
message. At fixed fusion-neutron power, a smaller first-wall area means a harsher wall
loading. Nuclear performance therefore couples the two plasma dimensions $R_0$ and
$a$ even before we make a plasma-transport calculation.

The more immediate geometric consequence of $b$ is radial crowding. On the inboard
side, the blanket lies between the plasma edge and the TF coil. It displaces the coil
toward the symmetry axis, where a toroidal field is stronger. The next section turns
that qualitative statement into an equation.

\designconsequence{The nuclear build $b$ separates plasma from magnet. That separation changes the attainable field through the inboard geometry.}

\section*{46. The field gets stronger as the coil moves inward}

The continuous-coil model already gave
\[
 B_\varphi(R)=\frac{\mu_0NI_c}{2\pi R}.
\]
At the plasma centre $R=R_0$, write this as $B_0$. The inboard edge of the
plasma is at $R_0-a$. After the nuclear island and an inboard TF-coil build have been
added, the innermost high-field location lies approximately at
\begin{equation}
 R_{\rm in}=R_0-a-b-c.
 \label{eq:rin}
\end{equation}
The sign is important: moving inward means decreasing $R$, which increases the
toroidal field for fixed coil current.

Using $B_\varphi(R)R=B_0R_0$, the maximum field in this simplified radial build is
\begin{equation}
 B_{\max}=\frac{B_0R_0}{R_0-a-b-c}.
 \label{eq:bmax}
\end{equation}
Equivalently,
\begin{equation}
 B_0=B_{\max}\left(1-\frac{a+b+c}{R_0}\right).
 \label{eq:b0radialbuild}
\end{equation}
This is the first compact expression of the radial-build problem. A larger plasma
minor radius, a thicker nuclear island, or a thicker TF coil leaves less inboard
radius. At fixed material limit $B_{\max}$, each reduces the attainable central field
$B_0$.

The equation also explains why a tokamak cannot be designed by choosing a desirable
$B_0$ and adding the blanket and magnets afterwards. Those supposedly external
components feed back directly into the field available to the plasma.

\samplefigure{\includegraphics[width=.88\linewidth]{figures/final/figS_inboard_build.pdf}}{The inboard radial ledger connects nuclear thickness $b$ and magnet thickness $c$ to the field available at the plasma. Dimensions shown are illustrative.\label{fig:figS_inboard_build}}

\section*{47. A field coil must carry current and survive its own force}

A magnetic field stores energy. In vacuum its energy density is
\begin{equation}
 u_B=\frac{B^2}{2\mu_0}.
 \label{eq:magnetic-energy-density}
\end{equation}
One way to obtain this expression is to build a solenoid gradually. Its magnetic
energy is $U=\tfrac12LI^2$; dividing by volume and substituting the ideal-solenoid
relations $L=\mu_0N^2A/\ell$ and $B=\mu_0NI/\ell$ gives
$U/(A\ell)=B^2/(2\mu_0)$.

The same scale appears as a magnetic pressure. If a field-filled region expands by a
small volume $dV$ at fixed flux, the released magnetic energy supplies mechanical
work $p_BdV$. The resulting stress scale is
\begin{equation}
 p_B=\frac{B^2}{2\mu_0}.
 \label{eq:magnetic-pressure}
\end{equation}
At high field this is enormous: the dependence is quadratic, so doubling field
quadruples this stress scale. A TF coil is therefore not just superconducting cable.
It needs a structural load path that prevents the winding pack from being crushed,
pulled apart, or driven beyond allowable strain.

For a transparent thickness estimate, let a representative magnetic force be
$F\sim p_BA_{\rm load}$ and let structural material of cross-sectional area
$A_{\rm str}$ be limited to stress $\sigma_{\rm allow}$. Force balance requires
\[
 \sigma_{\rm allow}A_{\rm str}\gtrsim p_BA_{\rm load}.
\]
If the geometry converts these areas into a representative support length $\ell_s$,
then the structural contribution obeys the scaling
\begin{equation}
 c_{\rm str}\sim
 \frac{B_{\max}^2}{2\mu_0\sigma_{\rm allow}}\,\ell_s
 \times(\hbox{geometry factor}).
 \label{eq:cstructure-scaling}
\end{equation}
The geometry factor incorporates tensile, centering, bending and wedging loads.
It connects the field-pressure scale to the required support thickness.

\samplescope{The support-thickness estimate uses a representative load path. Detailed sizing
requires the coil geometry and material allowables at the operating temperature and fluence.}

\section*{48. The other part of coil thickness: enough current-carrying area}

Stress is not the only reason a coil occupies space. Amp\`ere's law requires a total
enclosed current approximately
\begin{equation}
 NI\simeq\frac{2\pi R_{\rm in}B_{\max}}{\mu_0}.
 \label{eq:tf-ampere-turns}
\end{equation}
The product $NI$, called ampere-turns, cannot be carried through zero area. If
$J_{\rm wp}$ is the allowed current density averaged over the complete winding pack
(including conductor, stabilizer and internal pack allowances), the
required current-carrying area scales as
\begin{equation}
 A_{\rm wp}\gtrsim\frac{NI}{J_{\rm wp}}.
 \label{eq:winding-area}
\end{equation}
For a representative coil height $h_{\rm wp}$, this yields a winding-pack thickness
scale
\begin{equation}
 c_{\rm wp}\sim\frac{2\pi R_{\rm in}B_{\max}}
 {\mu_0J_{\rm wp}h_{\rm wp}}.
 \label{eq:winding-thickness}
\end{equation}
The area-to-thickness conversion uses the representative height $h_{\rm wp}$.
Its geometric factor depends on how the total ampere-turns are distributed among coils.

The full primary parameter is therefore a radial ledger:
\begin{equation}
 c=c_{\rm str}+c_{\rm wp}+c_{\rm ins}+c_{\rm cool}+c_{\rm margin}.
 \label{eq:coil-build}
\end{equation}
Here $c_{\rm str}$ denotes support outside the winding pack. The remaining
allowances cover insulation, cooling and margins outside that pack; internal
allowances already included in $J_{\rm wp}$ are counted only once. The first two
terms, Eqs.~\eqref{eq:cstructure-scaling} and \eqref{eq:winding-thickness}, are the
two irreducible physical demands: survive the field and produce the field.

\section*{49. Higher field changes both performance and magnet build}

We can now say precisely what a higher-field magnet changes. At fixed radial geometry,
Eq.~\eqref{eq:b0radialbuild} makes a higher allowable $B_{\max}$ available as a higher
central field $B_0$. But Eqs.~\eqref{eq:cstructure-scaling} and
\eqref{eq:winding-thickness} also show the price: field increases magnetic stress
quadratically and imposes a technology-dependent current-density requirement. Both effects enter the magnet-build calculation.

High-temperature superconductors (HTS) enter the argument after the baseline
closure identifies a field requirement beyond the conventional magnet model.
They enter this trade through the field and
current density their conductors can sustain. Their value to a reactor depends on
the resulting plasma performance, magnet build and engineering cost. The reference
calculation will compare field choices within the same coupled constraints.

\designconsequence{A higher allowable coil field can raise $B_0$, while stress and current-density requirements consume radial space. The benefit requires a coupled calculation.}

 P_E=\eta_TP_F.

\section*{53. Power and wall loading turn two dimensions into one family}

For the shaped boundary, the source uses the standard mean-square ellipse-perimeter
approximation. Revolving it around the major radius gives the first-wall area
\begin{equation}
 S_{\rm wall}\simeq4\pi^2R_0a
 \left(\frac{1+\kappa^2}{2}\right)^{1/2}.
 \label{eq:shaped-wall-area}
\end{equation}
This agrees with the circular result $4\pi^2R_0a$ when $\kappa=1$. 

The D--T neutron carries $E_n=14.1\,\mathrm{MeV}$ of the fusion energy
$E_{\rm DT}=17.6\,\mathrm{MeV}$. With $E_F$ the credited thermal energy per fusion reaction in the reference accounting,
\[
 P_n=\frac{E_n}{E_F}P_F
 =\frac{E_n}{E_F}\frac{P_E}{\eta_T}.
\]
Equating neutron power to the allowed wall loading times the wall area,
$P_n=W_nS_{\rm wall}$, gives
\begin{equation}
 R_0a=
 \frac{E_nP_E}
 {4\pi^2E_FW_n\eta_T}
 \left(\frac{2}{1+\kappa^2}\right)^{1/2}.
 \label{eq:r0a-wall-loading}
\end{equation}
At fixed mission, energy accounting and elongation, the right-hand side is constant:

\begin{equation}
 R_0(a)=\frac{C_{\rm wall}}{a},
 \qquad
 C_{\rm wall}\equiv
 \frac{E_nP_E}{4\pi^2E_FW_n\eta_T}
 \left(\frac{2}{1+\kappa^2}\right)^{1/2}.
 \label{eq:r0-of-a}
\end{equation}

\section*{55. Fusion power fixes the required pressure}

The D--T reaction rate in an equal fuel mixture is
\[
 \mathcal R=\frac{n^2}{4}\langle\sigma v\rangle(T),
\]
because $n_D=n_T=n/2$. Multiplying by the energy released per reaction and integrating
over plasma volume gives
\begin{equation}
 P_{\rm fus}=
 \frac{\langle\sigma v\rangle(T)E_{\rm DT}}{4}
 \int n^2\,dV.
 \label{eq:baseline-fusion-power}
\end{equation}
For a first transparent estimate, use volume-averaged $n$ and $T$:
\[
 P_{\rm fus}\simeq
 \frac{\langle\sigma v\rangle(T)E_{\rm DT}}{4}\,n^2V.
\]
The equal-temperature quasineutral model gave $p=2nT$, hence
\begin{equation}
 P_{\rm fus}\simeq
 \frac{\langle\sigma v\rangle(T)E_{\rm DT}}{16T^2}\,p^2V.
 \label{eq:baseline-power-pressure}
\end{equation}
Power balance therefore determines a required pressure:
\begin{equation}
 p_{\rm req}\simeq
 \left[
 \frac{16T^2P_{\rm fus,req}}
 {\langle\sigma v\rangle(T)E_{\rm DT}V}
 \right]^{1/2}.
 \label{eq:preq}
\end{equation}
The mission and available volume therefore demand a pressure. The reference model
uses $T\simeq14\,\mathrm{keV}$, near the broad favourable range of
$\langle\sigma v\rangle/T^2$ for D--T.

\samplemodel{The estimate above uses uniform density and temperature. The numerical
reference calculation uses the declared profiles introduced in \S68.}

\designconsequence{Fusion output fixes a pressure requirement. Alpha heating must
then sustain the thermal energy stored at that pressure.}

\section*{56. Ignition turns pressure into a required confinement time}

The neutron carries energy out of the plasma; for D--T,
the alpha fraction is
\[
 \frac{E_\alpha}{E_{\rm DT}}=\frac{3.5}{17.6}\simeq\frac15.
\]
For an ignited plasma, alpha heating must replace the energy conducted and transported
out of the core. Let $W$ be thermal energy stored in the plasma and define
\[
 \tau_E=\frac{W}{P_{\rm loss}}.
\]
The ignition balance is $P_\alpha=P_{\rm loss}=W/\tau_E$, or
\begin{equation}
 \tau_{E,\rm req}=\frac{W}{P_\alpha}.
 \label{eq:taue-required}
\end{equation}

In the equal-temperature model, the thermal energy density is
$w=\tfrac32(n_e+n_i)T=3nT=\tfrac32p$. Thus $W\simeq\tfrac32pV$. With
$P_\alpha\simeq P_{\rm fus}/5$,
\begin{equation}
 \tau_{E,\rm req}\simeq
 \frac{15pV}{2P_{\rm fus}}.
 \label{eq:taue-pressure}
\end{equation}
\designconsequence{Power and wall loading determine the required pressure and
confinement time. The regression will translate that time into a current demand.}

\section*{57. Before testing a limit: what kind of statement is it?}

Three kinds of evidence enter the four tests:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{p{0.22\textwidth}p{0.66\textwidth}}
\textbf{Derived relation} & follows from stated equations and stated approximations;
for example, $p=2nT$ and $q_*\propto a^2B_0/(R_0I)$.\\[3pt]
\textbf{Physics model} & follows after adopting a specified equilibrium, profile, or
kinetic model; its regime and profile assumptions accompany the result.\\[3pt]
\textbf{Empirical boundary} & summarizes experimental operation or a regression; its coefficient and applicability range come from that evidence.
\end{tabular}

\end{center}

Greenwald density is empirical; the practical Troyon coefficient combines stability
modelling and experimental evidence. The kink threshold depends on the adopted MHD
model, profiles and wall. Bootstrap current requires a kinetic closure and compatible
profiles.

For every constraint define a demand-to-allowance ratio,
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal R\equiv\frac{\hbox{quantity demanded by the reference design}}
 {\hbox{quantity allowed by the stated constraint}}.
 \label{eq:constraint-ratio}
\end{equation}
All relevant ratios must be at most one at the same design point.

\section*{58. The one empirical input that determines the required current}

The 2015 reference case uses an ELMy H-mode confinement regression. H-mode has a strong edge transport barrier. ELMy identifies discharges with recurring
edge-localized-mode relaxations, which expel edge energy and particles.\footnote{A

the associated edge-localized modes: \url{https://www.iter.org/node/20687/progress-elm-physics-and-elm-control}.}
In the declared units, the regression is

\tau_E =
0.145H\,
\frac{I^{0.93}R_0^{1.39}a^{0.58}\kappa^{0.78}
\bar n^{0.41}B_0^{0.15}A^{0.19}}
{P_\alpha^{0.69}}
\quad\mathrm{s}.
\label{eq:freidberg-confinement}
\end{equation}
Here $I$ is in MA, $R_0$ and $a$ in m, $\bar n$ in
$10^{20}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}$, $B_0$ in T, $P_\alpha$ in MW, $A=2.5$ is the
mean ion mass used by the fit, and $H$ is the confinement multiplier. The coefficient belongs to these units and the selected regression.
Here $A$ denotes mean ion mass; the geometric aspect ratio is written explicitly as $R_0/a$.

\samplescope{This is the empirical closure in Freidberg, Mangiarotti and Minervini
(2015), Eq.~(28). Treat the regression as data with a stated applicability range.
Its current, size and power exponents describe the fitted operating regime.}

The conventional baseline uses $H=1$; varying $H$ later tests a specified improvement
in confinement.

\section*{59. Solving the confinement fit for the current}

The ignition calculation has already supplied $\tau_{E,\rm req}$. Set it equal to the
right side of Eq.~\eqref{eq:freidberg-confinement} and isolate the only unknown in
this step, $I$:
\[
 I^{0.93}=
\frac{\tau_{E,\rm req}P_\alpha^{0.69}}
{0.145H R_0^{1.39}a^{0.58}\kappa^{0.78}
\bar n^{0.41}B_0^{0.15}A^{0.19}}.
\]
Raising both sides to the power $1/0.93$ gives
\begin{equation}
 I_{\rm req}=
\left[
\frac{\tau_{E,\rm req}P_\alpha^{0.69}}
{0.145H R_0^{1.39}a^{0.58}\kappa^{0.78}
\bar n^{0.41}B_0^{0.15}A^{0.19}}
\right]^{1/0.93}\mathrm{MA}.
\label{eq:ireq-from-confinement}
\end{equation}
This current makes the fitted confinement time equal the ignition requirement.

After substituting the reference design's previously determined relations, Freidberg
obtains the compact scaling
\begin{equation}
 I_{\rm req}\simeq
 12.1\,\frac{a^{1.63}}{B_0^{0.16}}\ \mathrm{MA},
\label{eq:ireq-compact}
\end{equation}
The coefficient absorbs the fixed baseline inputs and stated units. Current grows
strongly with $a$ and depends weakly on $B_0$ in this reduced relation. At
$a=1.34\,\mathrm m$ and $B_0=6.83\,\mathrm T$, the source obtains
$I_{\rm req}=14.3\,\mathrm{MA}$.

\designconsequence{The confinement requirement fixes the current. Its poloidal field
must leave sufficient field-line twist for the adopted stability criterion.}

\section*{60. High required current immediately tests field-line twist}

The safety factor was introduced as the number of toroidal circuits made by a field
line per poloidal circuit. At the plasma edge, more plasma current produces more
poloidal field, so it reduces this twist count. For the elongated reference geometry,
the paper uses
\begin{equation}
 q_*=
\frac{2\pi a^2B_0}{\mu_0R_0I}
\left(\frac{1+\kappa^2}{2}\right).
\label{eq:freidberg-qstar}
\end{equation}
The first factor is the Amp\`ere-law estimate already derived from
$B_\theta\sim\mu_0I/(2\pi a)$; the shape factor records the paper's chosen elongation
correction. Thus substituting $I_{\rm req}$, not an independently chosen current,
produces the required $q_*$.

A helical displacement can release magnetic energy associated with the plasma current.
The ideal-MHD kink criterion depends on current and pressure profiles, wall response,
rotation and mode number.

The
reference model uses the practical external-kink/disruption condition
\begin{equation}
 q_*\ge q_K,\qquad q_K\simeq2.
\label{eq:kink-boundary}
\end{equation}
\samplemodel{The adopted threshold represents a practical external-kink/disruption
boundary. A disruption is an abrupt loss of controlled equilibrium and confinement,
often followed by rapid current termination and large transient loads.}

The ratio is $\mathcal R_K=q_K/q_*$: raising the demanded current lowers $q_*$ and
increases this ratio.

\section*{65. Fourth audit: the required bootstrap share}

A steady-state tokamak cannot rely on a transformer indefinitely. Its current ledger
must be
\[
 I=I_{\rm CD}+I_{\rm bs},
\]
where $I_{\rm CD}$ is externally driven current and $I_{\rm bs}$ is bootstrap current.
Divide by the required total current:
\begin{equation}
 1=\frac{I_{\rm CD}}{I}+f_B,
 \qquad
 f_B=1-\frac{I_{\rm CD}}{I}.
\label{eq:required-bootstrap}
\end{equation}
This is a current accounting identity; the two sources must supply its terms.

For the reference recirculating-power and lower-hybrid current-drive model, the paper
obtains $I_{\rm CD}=2.25\,\mathrm{MA}$. With $I=14.3\,\mathrm{MA}$,
\begin{equation}
 f_B=1-\frac{2.25}{14.3}=0.84.
\label{eq:required-bootstrap-reference}
\end{equation}
A large total current and limited external-drive allowance demand a large bootstrap
share. The attainable fraction $f_{\rm NC}$ follows from the kinetic closure,
pressure profiles and magnetic geometry.

\designconsequence{The ledger requires $f_B=0.84$. Trapped-particle dynamics and
radial gradients determine how much self-current the plasma can supply.}

\section*{66. Where bootstrap current actually comes from}

Sections~36--37 introduced trapped particles and an outward-decreasing pressure
profile. Their coupling provides the bootstrap drive.

In a uniform straight field, particles moving parallel to $\mathbf B$ in the two
directions can cancel one another's current. In a torus, trapped particles bounce
between mirror points and do not complete a full parallel circuit. A pressure gradient
means that the population arriving from the core side of a narrow radial shell is not
identical to the population arriving from the edge side. Grad-$B$ and curvature drifts
then separate the two populations slightly. Collisions transfer part of that imbalance
between trapped and passing particles. The cancellation of parallel electron motion is
therefore incomplete: a toroidal current remains.

The pressure profile supplies the free energy. Flattening the density and temperature
profiles removes this drive; steepening them increases the drive and the associated
stability burden. Bootstrap current belongs in the same closure calculation as beta
and kink safety.

\samplefigure{\includegraphics[width=.92\linewidth]{figures/final/figS_bootstrap_from_trapped.pdf}}{Trapped orbits, radial gradients and collisional coupling produce a parallel-current imbalance. The kinetic closure quantifies its magnitude.\label{fig:figS_bootstrap_from_trapped}}

\designconsequence{Pressure gradients can supply part of the required current.
Quantifying that share requires a kinetic closure on the same profiles and geometry.}

\section*{67. The kinetic closure for bootstrap current}

The mathematical object behind the preceding paragraph is a drift-kinetic equation.
In schematic form for a distribution function $f(\mathbf x,\mathbf v)$ it is
\begin{equation}
 \left(v_\parallel\mathbf b+\mathbf v_d\right)\!\cdot\!\nabla f
 +\frac{qE_\parallel}{m}\frac{\partial f}{\partial v_\parallel}
 =C[f].
 \label{eq:drift-kinetic-schematic}
\end{equation}
Here $\mathbf b=\mathbf B/B$, $\mathbf v_d$ represents the guiding-center drifts,
and $C[f]$ is the Coulomb collision operator. Trapped orbits and collisional coupling enter through this kinetic description.

Freidberg, Mangiarotti, and Minervini therefore choose a particular large-aspect-ratio,
low-collisionality neoclassical closure. They assume equal ion and electron
temperatures, $T_i=T_e=T$, one effective ion charge, $Z=1$, and a circular
area-equivalent radius
\begin{equation}
 \hat a=\kappa^{1/2}a,\qquad \rho=\frac r{\hat a}.
 \label{eq:ahat-rho}
\end{equation}
Their local bootstrap-current model is their Eq.~(41):
\begin{equation}
 J_B(\rho)=-2.44\left(\frac r{R_0}\right)^{1/2}\frac p{B_\theta}
 \left(\frac1n\frac{dn}{dr}+0.055\,\frac1T\frac{dT}{dr}\right).
 \label{eq:freidberg-local-bootstrap}
\end{equation}
\samplemodel{Equation~\eqref{eq:freidberg-local-bootstrap} is the source's Eq.~(41),
a large-aspect-ratio, low-collisionality closure for $T_i=T_e$ and $Z=1$.
Its coefficients include the kinetic response. Gradients, poloidal field and the area
integral are evaluated explicitly below.}

\section*{68. The selected profiles make the pressure-gradient term calculable}

The same paper does not leave $n(r)$ and $T(r)$ implicit. Its standard-profile choice
(its Eq.~(20)) is
\begin{equation}
 T=2\bar T(1-\rho^2),\qquad
 p=2.5\bar p(1-\rho^2)^{3/2},\qquad
 n=1.5\bar n(1-\rho^2)^{1/2}.
 \label{eq:freidberg-profiles}
\end{equation}
The numerical factors make the bars cross-sectional averages. For example,
$\int_0^1 2\rho\,[2\bar T(1-\rho^2)]\,d\rho=\bar T$.

Because $d\rho/dr=1/\hat a$, ordinary differentiation gives
\begin{align}
 \frac1n\frac{dn}{dr}
 &=\frac{1}{1.5\bar n(1-\rho^2)^{1/2}}
 \left[1.5\bar n\,\frac12(1-\rho^2)^{-1/2}
       \left(-2\rho\right)\frac1{\hat a}\right]
 =-\frac{\rho}{\hat a(1-\rho^2)},\\
 \frac1T\frac{dT}{dr}
 &=\frac{1}{2\bar T(1-\rho^2)}
 \left[2\bar T\left(-2\rho\right)\frac1{\hat a}\right]
 =-\frac{2\rho}{\hat a(1-\rho^2)}.
 \label{eq:profile-log-gradients}
\end{align}
Thus the bracket in Eq.~\eqref{eq:freidberg-local-bootstrap} is
\begin{equation}
 \frac1n\frac{dn}{dr}+0.055\frac1T\frac{dT}{dr}
 =-\frac{(1+0.110)\rho}{\hat a(1-\rho^2)}.
 \label{eq:bootstrap-gradient-combination}
\end{equation}
Substitution of Eqs.~\eqref{eq:freidberg-profiles} and
\eqref{eq:bootstrap-gradient-combination} into Eq.~\eqref{eq:freidberg-local-bootstrap}
then gives, with $2.44(1.110)(2.5)=6.77\simeq6.8$,
\begin{equation}
 J_B(\rho)=6.8\,\frac{\bar p}{\hat a^{1/2}R_0^{1/2}}
 \frac{\rho^{3/2}(1-\rho^2)^{1/2}}{B_\theta(\rho)}.
 \label{eq:bootstrap-profiled-current}
\end{equation}
The direction follows the conventions for $B_\theta$ and current. The radial shape
also depends on $B_\theta(\rho)$, which comes from the total-current profile.

\section*{69. The poloidal field is obtained from the assumed total-current profile}

The total-current profile determines the remaining denominator $B_\theta(\rho)$.
The paper selects

\begin{equation}
 J_T(\rho)=\frac{I}{\pi\hat a^2}\frac{9\rho^{1/4}}8
 \frac{\alpha^2(1-x)e^{\alpha x}}{e^\alpha-1-\alpha},
 \qquad x=\rho^{9/4},\quad\alpha=2.53.
 \label{eq:freidberg-total-current-profile}
\end{equation}
This is another profile assumption, chosen to put appreciable current away from the
axis. It is normalized correctly: using $\rho=x^{4/9}$ gives
\begin{equation}
 \rho^{1/4}\rho\,d\rho=\frac49\,dx,
 \qquad
 \frac{I_{\rm enc}}I
 =\int_0^x\frac{\alpha^2(1-u)e^{\alpha u}}{e^\alpha-1-\alpha}\,du.
 \label{eq:enclosed-current-step}
\end{equation}
The antiderivative is
\begin{equation}
 \int\alpha^2(1-u)e^{\alpha u}\,du
 =\left(1+\alpha-\alpha u\right)e^{\alpha u},
 \label{eq:current-profile-antiderivative}
\end{equation}
so evaluation between $0$ and $x$ yields
\begin{equation}
 \frac{I_{\rm enc}}I=
 \frac{(1+\alpha-\alpha x)e^{\alpha x}-1-\alpha}{e^\alpha-1-\alpha}.
 \label{eq:enclosed-current}
\end{equation}
Ampere's law $2\pi rB_\theta=\mu_0I_{\rm enc}$ now gives
\begin{equation}
 b_\theta(\rho)\equiv\frac{B_\theta}{\mu_0I/(2\pi\hat a)}
 =\frac1\rho\left[
 \frac{(1+\alpha-\alpha x)e^{\alpha x}-1-\alpha}{e^\alpha-1-\alpha}
 \right].
 \label{eq:freidberg-btheta}
\end{equation}
At $\rho=1$, $I_{\rm enc}=I$ and $b_\theta=1$, confirming the normalization.
Near the axis the selected profile gives $b_\theta\propto\rho^{5/4}$, so the bootstrap
current density scales as $\rho^{1/4}$ and remains regular.

\designconsequence{The total-current profile fixes the poloidal field. Integrating
the resulting bootstrap density gives the available current share.}

\section*{70. Integrate the local current, then test the steady-state requirement}

In the area-equivalent circular cross-section, $dA=2\pi\hat a^2\rho\,d\rho$ after
integrating over the polar angle. Thus
\begin{align}
 I_B&=2\pi\hat a^2\int_0^1J_B(\rho)\rho\,d\rho\\
 &=2\pi\hat a^2\int_0^1
 \left[6.8\frac{\bar p}{\hat a^{1/2}R_0^{1/2}}
 \frac{\rho^{3/2}(1-\rho^2)^{1/2}}{B_\theta}\right]\rho\,d\rho.
 \label{eq:bootstrap-area-integral}
\end{align}
Replace $B_\theta$ using Eq.~\eqref{eq:freidberg-btheta}, divide by $I$, and collect
constants. Since $4\pi^2(6.8)=268.5$, the result is the paper's Eq.~(44):
\begin{equation}
 f_{\rm NC}\equiv\frac{I_B}{I}
 =268\frac{\hat a^{5/2}\bar p}{\mu_0R_0^{1/2}I^2}
 \int_0^1\frac{\rho^{5/2}(1-\rho^2)^{1/2}}{b_\theta(\rho)}\,d\rho.
 \label{eq:freidberg-bootstrap-fraction}
\end{equation}
Numerical quadrature at $\alpha=2.53$ gives the dimensionless integral $0.318$
(to three significant figures).

For the reference point,
\begin{equation}
 \kappa=1.70,\quad \hat a=\sqrt{1.70}(1.34\,\mathrm m)=1.747\,\mathrm m,
 \quad \bar p=7.57\,\mathrm{atm},\quad R_0=5.34\,\mathrm m,\quad
 I=14.3\,\mathrm{MA},
 \label{eq:bootstrap-reference-inputs}
\end{equation}
where $1\,\mathrm{atm}=101325\,\mathrm{Pa}$ and $1\,\mathrm{MA}=10^6\,\mathrm A$.
Equation~\eqref{eq:freidberg-bootstrap-fraction} then gives
\begin{equation}
 f_{\rm NC}=0.44.
 \label{eq:bootstrap-reference-result}
\end{equation}
The required value from Eq.~\eqref{eq:required-bootstrap-reference} was $f_B=0.84$.
The audited comparison is therefore
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal R_B=\frac{f_B}{f_{\rm NC}}=\frac{0.84}{0.44}=1.91>1.
 \label{eq:bootstrap-reference-ratio}
\end{equation}
The selected standard-profile model supplies about half the required self-current.

 \label{eq:current-drive-power-scan}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
 \eta_{\rm CD}\equiv\frac{R_0\bar n I_{\rm CD}}{P_{\rm CD}}
 \simeq\frac{1.2}{n_\parallel^2}.
 \label{eq:freidberg-current-drive-efficiency}
\end{equation}

 \omega_{pe}^2=\frac{ne^2}{\epsilon_0m_e},\qquad

\end{equation}

\begin{equation}

 \label{eq:bootstrap-demand-scan}
\end{equation}

both kink safety and steady-state current supply.

A remedy must reduce the confinement-driven current or improve the available current
supply and twist margin while retaining the other allowances. The next tests vary
confinement ($H$), then the engineering field ceiling ($B_{\max}$), before changing
the plant mission and geometry.

\designconsequence{The baseline identifies the current-closure problem that improved
confinement and higher field must solve. HTS enters when the required field exceeds
the conventional magnet model.}

\section*{77. Remedy one: improved confinement}

The first remedy asks whether the plasma can retain energy longer than the reference
H-mode regression predicts:

\begin{equation}
 \tau_E=H\tau_{E,\rm ref},\qquad H>1.
 \label{eq:h-factor-definition}
\end{equation}
Here $H$ measures confinement relative to the regression in \S58. Pressure and
current-profile control can change the magnetic shear and flow environment of turbulent
eddies; transport barriers can reduce outward heat loss. Maintaining such a state
requires control compatible with alpha heating in an ignited plasma.

The confinement multiplier $H$ is evaluated against a specified regression; these profiles illustrate transport control rather than a predicted reactor operating point.\label{fig:figS_h_factor}}

\samplescope{An assumed $H>1$ specifies the confinement performance required of the
scenario. Its sustained value must be demonstrated in the relevant operating regime.}

\section*{78. Better confinement lowers current---and moves every coupled test}

Equation~\eqref{eq:ireq-from-confinement} contains $H$ only in the denominator
inside a power $1/0.93$. Holding the other inputs of that equation fixed gives
\begin{equation}
 I_{\rm req}(H)=I_{\rm req}(1)\,H^{-1/0.93}.
 \label{eq:h-current-scaling}
\end{equation}
Thus a larger $H$ lowers the current that the empirical fit demands. Since
$q_*\propto1/I$ at fixed geometry,
\begin{equation}
 q_*(H)=q_*(1)\,H^{1/0.93}.
 \label{eq:h-q-scaling}
\end{equation}
This is the intended benefit: less current produces more field-line twist and hence
more margin in the adopted kink test.

The same reduced current tightens two allowance ratios. The Greenwald allowance
is proportional to $I$, and the adopted Troyon pressure allowance is also
proportional to $I$. At fixed geometry and fusion pressure,
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal R_G(H)=\mathcal R_G(1)H^{1/0.93},\qquad
 \mathcal R_T(H)=\mathcal R_T(1)H^{1/0.93}.
 \label{eq:h-density-beta-scaling}
\end{equation}
In words: lowering current helps kink safety but makes a fixed density and pressure
look larger relative to current-based operating boundaries.

Bootstrap closure shifts in the helpful direction at fixed geometry. From
Eq.~\eqref{eq:freidberg-bootstrap-fraction}, $f_{\rm NC}\propto p/I^2$, while
$f_B=1-I_{\rm CD}/I$. Lower $I$ both raises the calculated bootstrap fraction and
reduces the required bootstrap share if the current-drive allowance is held fixed.
\designconsequence{Lower current helps kink safety and bootstrap closure while
reducing density and beta allowances. The remedy requires a simultaneous recalculation.}

\begin{equation}
 f_B\bigl(a,H\bigr)=f_{\rm NC}\bigl(a,H\bigr).
 \label{eq:h-bootstrap-match}
\end{equation}

\section*{81. Freidberg's second remedy: raise the field ceiling}

The second remedy keeps $H=1$ and raises the maximum inboard TF-coil field
$B_{\max}$. Using the source's analytic radial-build convention from \S54,

\begin{equation}
 B_0=B_{\max}(1-\epsilon_B).
 \label{eq:high-field-b0}
\end{equation}
At fixed fractional build, the central field rises in proportion. The mission still
requires the same pressure, and

\begin{equation}
 \beta=\frac{2\mu_0p}{B_0^2}.
 \label{eq:high-field-beta-definition}
\end{equation}
Thus at fixed pressure and geometry, doubling $B_0$ quarters $\beta$. This is the
first reason high field can relieve a pressure-stability problem: it increases the
magnetic pressure available to balance the same plasma pressure.

The reference compact expression for beta had $13\,\mathrm T$ absorbed into its
numerical coefficient. Restore that dependence when varying $B_{\max}$:
\begin{equation}
 \beta(\%)=
 \frac{1.31}{a^{1/2}(1-\epsilon_B)^2}
 \left(\frac{13\,\mathrm T}{B_{\max}}\right)^2.
 \label{eq:high-field-beta-scan}
\end{equation}
This is the beta definition with the source's power-and-size relations substituted.
The explicit field factor updates the coefficient when moving away from $13\,\mathrm T$.
$B_{\max}$ remains a supporting engineering input: it changes $B_0$, coil thickness
$c$ and the coupled plasma requirements.

\section*{82. Higher field changes plasma and mechanical requirements}

First hold geometry fixed as a thought experiment. The selected confinement regression
gave the compact current requirement
\[
 I_{\rm req}\propto B_0^{-0.16}.
\]
This direct reduction in demanded current is weak. The full benefit requires
recalculating safety factor, drive efficiency, bootstrap and radial build together.

The safety-factor relation makes one helpful direction visible without any empirical
claim:
\begin{equation}
 q_*\propto\frac{a^2B_0}{R_0I}.
 \label{eq:high-field-q-direction}
\end{equation}
Higher toroidal field and lower current both raise $q_*$ at fixed geometry.
The same field imposes a mechanical load:

\begin{equation}
 u_B=p_B=\frac{B_{\max}^2}{2\mu_0}.
 \label{eq:high-field-stress}
\end{equation}
For a fixed allowable stress, the higher Lorentz load requires more structure.
The analytic coil model calculates the corresponding thickness $c$.

\designconsequence{Higher field lowers beta and raises twist, while magnetic pressure
grows as $B_{\max}^2$. The scan must retain the accompanying structural demand.}

\section*{83. Why field also changes the selected current-drive calculation}

The reference current ledger limits externally driven current because wave power is
limited. Its lower-hybrid model does not treat current-drive efficiency as a constant:
\begin{equation}
 \eta_{\rm CD}\simeq\frac{1.2}{n_\parallel^2},\qquad
 I_{\rm CD}=\frac{P_{\rm CD}\eta_{\rm CD}}{R_0\bar n}.
 \label{eq:high-field-current-drive}
\end{equation}
Here $n_\parallel\equiv ck_\parallel/\omega$ is the parallel refractive index of the
launched wave, with $c$ here denoting the speed of light. In the selected outside-launch approximation it contains the ratio
\begin{equation}
 \frac{\omega_{pe}}{\Omega_e},\qquad
 \omega_{pe}^2=\frac{ne^2}{\epsilon_0m_e},\qquad
 \Omega_e=\frac{eB}{m_e}.
 \label{eq:high-field-frequency-ratio}
\end{equation}
Here $\omega_{pe}$ is the electron plasma frequency and $\Omega_e$ is the electron cyclotron frequency. At fixed density, increasing local $B$ increases
$\Omega_e$ and decreases $\omega_{pe}/\Omega_e$. In this particular lower-hybrid
launch model, the resulting change in $n_\parallel$ improves the available
current-drive efficiency. That improvement reduces the bootstrap share required by
the ledger
\[
 f_B=1-\frac{I_{\rm CD}}{I}.
\]

\samplemodel{The scan retains the source's $40\,\mathrm{MW}$ wave-power allocation,
lower-hybrid accessibility model, profiles and $H=1$. It varies $B_{\max}$ and repeats
the coupled calculation. The current-drive benefit depends on this launch and damping
model; other wave systems require their own calculation.}

\section*{85. The price is measurable: more engineered volume and more exhaust burden}

The increasing $c$ column is already a direct mechanical cost of high field. The
source also records two simple engineering figures of merit. Its engineered-volume

\begin{equation}
 \frac{V_I}{P_E}=\frac{V_B+V_{\rm TF}}{P_E},
 \label{eq:freidberg-cost-proxy}
\end{equation}
Here $V_B$ and $V_{\rm TF}$ are blanket and TF-coil volumes in the specified geometry.
The ratio measures engineered material per electric megawatt.

The source's parallel-heat-flux proxy follows the alpha power along the field:
\begin{equation}
 Q_\parallel=\frac{P_\alpha B_0}{R_0}.
 \label{eq:freidberg-parallel-heat-proxy}
\end{equation}

\section*{86. HTS is earned here: an enabler with a mechanical bill}

A superconductor carries current with negligible DC resistance below its operating
critical surface. Its usable current depends on field, temperature, angle, strain
and conductor architecture:

\[
 I_c=I_c(B,T,\theta,\varepsilon,\hbox{conductor architecture}).
\]
The conductor must remain below that surface throughout normal operation and credible
transients, while its stabilizer, insulation, joints where present, cryogenic system,
and protection system handle disturbances.

The scan gives a concrete requirement: this $H=1$ route needs a coil-field ceiling
near $17.6\,\mathrm T$, above the conventional $13\,\mathrm T$ baseline.

Rare-earth barium copper oxide (REBCO), commonly called an HTS conductor, is a serious
candidate because it can retain useful current capability in fields and temperatures
where a conventional low-temperature-superconductor design is much more constrained.
This is no longer purely aspirational: a representative-scale REBCO TF model coil
was built and tested to $20.1\,\mathrm T$ peak field on conductor at $20\,\mathrm K$.
\footnote{Z. S. Hartwig \emph{et al.}, The SPARC Toroidal Field Model Coil
Program, open preprint, 2023: \url{https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.12301}. The report
also records $40.5\,\mathrm{kA}$ terminal current, large Lorentz loading, and nearly
$1\,\mathrm{GPa}$ structural-case stress.}

HTS provides access to higher field while the magnetic-pressure load in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:high-field-stress} remains. Magnet design must close stress and strain,
quench detection and energy extraction, radiation tolerance, joint resistance,
manufacturing, cryogenic power and replacement.

\designconsequence{The high-field calculation gives HTS a specific role and operating
requirement. The next remedy keeps the conventional field ceiling and changes plant output.}

\section*{87. Freidberg's third remedy: change the plant scale}

The third remedy raises electric output $P_E$ from $1000\,\mathrm{MW_e}$.
At fixed neutron wall loading, additional fusion power requires more wall area:

\begin{equation}
 R_0a=
 7.16\left(\frac{P_E}{1000\,\mathrm{MW}}\right)\mathrm{m^2},
 \label{eq:power-scan-wall-product}
\end{equation}
where the coefficient retains the 2015 blanket, shape, wall-loading, and thermal
conversion assumptions.

At fixed $a$, $R_0$ and plasma volume rise with output. The plant gains toroidal
length while retaining the assigned neutron power per square metre.

For this test Freidberg holds the original plasma and magnet assumptions:
\[
 H=1,\qquad B_{\max}=13\,\mathrm T,\qquad W_n=4\,\mathrm{MW\,m^{-2}},
\]
and again uses $f_B=f_{\rm NC}$ to select the compatible $a$ for each trial output.
The test asks whether scale alone produces overlap of the four constraints.

\section*{88. Output and volume grow together at fixed wall loading}

At the fixed temperature and profiles of the reference model, fusion power has the
form
\begin{equation}
 P_{\rm fus}\propto p^2V.
 \label{eq:power-scan-fusion-scaling}
\end{equation}
For a torus of fixed elongation, $V\propto R_0a^2$. Insert
Eq.~\eqref{eq:power-scan-wall-product}:
\[
 V\propto (R_0a)a\propto P_Ea.
\]
The thermal fusion power is proportional to $P_E$, so
\[
 P_E\propto p^2(P_Ea)
 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
 p\propto a^{-1/2}.
 \label{eq:power-scan-pressure-cancellation}
\]
The factor $P_E$ cancels. At fixed $a$, the mission asks for a proportionally larger
volume at proportionally larger power, leaving the required average pressure
unchanged. With the fixed $T\simeq14\,\mathrm{keV}$ model and $p=2nT$, the same is
true of the average density:
\[
 n\propto a^{-1/2}.
\]

The confinement time makes the cancellation visible a second way. The stored thermal
energy scales as $W\sim pV\propto P_Ea^{1/2}$, while alpha heating scales as
$P_\alpha\propto P_E$. Thus
\begin{equation}
 \tau_{E,\rm req}=\frac{W}{P_\alpha}\propto a^{1/2},
 \label{eq:power-scan-taue-cancellation}
\end{equation}
again with no direct $P_E$ dependence at fixed $a$.

\designconsequence{At fixed $a$, increased output requires proportionally more volume
at the same pressure. Any current benefit must come from the larger major radius in
the confinement and drive relations.}

Raising output at fixed wall loading preserves the required pressure; lowering wall loading at fixed output reduces it.\label{fig:figS_scale_two_ways}}

\section*{89. The enlarged machine changes the current requirement}

The selected confinement relation, solved for current in \S59, has the dependence
\[
 I^{0.93}\propto
 \frac{\tau_{E,\rm req}P_\alpha^{0.69}}
 {R_0^{1.39}a^{0.58}n^{0.41}B_0^{0.15}}.
\]
At fixed $a$, section~88 showed that $\tau_{E,\rm req}$ and $n$ do not carry
a direct power-output factor. In contrast,
\[
 P_\alpha^{0.69}\propto P_E^{0.69},
\qquad
 R_0^{1.39}\propto P_E^{1.39}.
\]
Ignoring for one line the smaller change in $B_0$ caused by the changed radial
geometry, their ratio is $P_E^{-0.70}$. Raising both sides to $1/0.93$ gives
\begin{equation}
 I_{\rm req}\propto P_E^{-0.70/0.93}
\simeq P_E^{-0.75}.
 \label{eq:power-scan-current-direction}
\end{equation}
This is the central mechanism of the power-output remedy. The larger major radius
helps the empirical confinement scaling more strongly than the increased alpha power
hurts it, so the regression asks for less current at a fixed cross-section.

The current-drive budget must be updated at the same time. If the selected
recirculating-power fraction remains fixed, $P_{\rm CD}\propto P_E$. But
\[
 I_{\rm CD}=\frac{P_{\rm CD}\eta_{\rm CD}}{R_0n}
\]
contains the same factor $R_0\propto P_E$ in its denominator. At fixed $a$ its direct
power dependence approximately cancels, leaving field and wave-accessibility changes
to determine the remainder. The required total current, however, falls according to
Eq.~\eqref{eq:power-scan-current-direction}. Therefore the bootstrap fraction
required by the ledger falls:
\[
 f_B=1-\frac{I_{\rm CD}}{I}.
\]
\samplescope{The exponent $-0.75$ follows from the selected regression, fixed wall
loading, fixed temperature and profiles, and the stated power budget. The full scan
also updates $B_0$ and selects $a$ through bootstrap matching.}

\section*{92. Freidberg's fourth remedy: lower the permitted wall loading}

The last single-knob remedy changes the allowed average neutron wall loading $W_n$.
The reference value was $4\,\mathrm{MW\,m^{-2}}$. Lowering it means demanding a
larger first-wall area for the same neutron power:
\begin{equation}
 R_0a=
 7.16\left(\frac{4\,\mathrm{MW\,m^{-2}}}{W_n}\right)\mathrm{m^2}
 \qquad(P_E=1000\,\mathrm{MW_e}).
 \label{eq:wall-scan-wall-product}
\end{equation}
Lower neutron power per unit area requires more first-wall surface at the same output.

The immediate trade is visible before any plasma fit is used. At fixed $a$,
$R_0\propto W_n^{-1}$ and $V\propto R_0a^2\propto W_n^{-1}$. The same fusion output
is produced in a larger volume. That lowers the required power density, pressure, and
density, but it makes the reactor physically larger. Freidberg holds
\[
 P_E=1000\,\mathrm{MW_e},\qquad H=1,\qquad B_{\max}=13\,\mathrm T,
\]
and again imposes bootstrap closure to select $a(W_n)$ before testing the four
ratios.

\designconsequence{Lower wall loading reduces required power density by increasing
wall area and volume. We next calculate how that change reaches the current requirement.}

\section*{93. Lower wall loading reduces the required pressure}

At fixed output and temperature, use again
\[
 P_{\rm fus}\propto p^2V.
\]
From Eq.~\eqref{eq:wall-scan-wall-product}, $V\propto W_n^{-1}a$ at fixed
elongation. Holding total fusion power fixed therefore gives
\begin{equation}
 p\propto\left(\frac{W_n}{a}\right)^{1/2},\qquad
 n\propto\left(\frac{W_n}{a}\right)^{1/2}.
 \label{eq:wall-scan-pressure-density}
\end{equation}
Here reducing $W_n$ reduces the average pressure and density required to make the same total power.

The stored energy scales as
\[
 W\sim pV\propto
\left(\frac{W_n}{a}\right)^{1/2}\left(\frac a{W_n}\right)
=\left(\frac a{W_n}\right)^{1/2}.
\]
The alpha power is unchanged because the total fusion output is unchanged. Hence
\begin{equation}
 \tau_{E,\rm req}\propto\left(\frac a{W_n}\right)^{1/2}.
 \label{eq:wall-scan-taue}
\end{equation}
The required confinement time rises. Its effect on current must be combined with
the larger $R_0$, lower density and changed field in the regression.
Figure~\ref{fig:figS_scale_two_ways} compares this route with increased output.

\section*{94. The net current direction follows from all terms together}

Use the same selected confinement dependence:
\[
 I^{0.93}\propto
\frac{\tau_{E,\rm req}P_\alpha^{0.69}}
{R_0^{1.39}a^{0.58}n^{0.41}B_0^{0.15}}.
\]
At fixed $a$, Eqs.~\eqref{eq:wall-scan-pressure-density} and
\eqref{eq:wall-scan-taue} give
\[
 \tau_{E,\rm req}\propto W_n^{-1/2},\qquad
 R_0^{-1.39}\propto W_n^{1.39},\qquad
 n^{-0.41}\propto W_n^{-0.205}.
\]
Multiplying the three explicit wall-loading powers gives
\[
 W_n^{-0.500+1.390-0.205}=W_n^{0.685}.
\]
Consequently,
\begin{equation}
 I_{\rm req}\propto W_n^{0.685/0.93}
\simeq W_n^{0.74},
 \label{eq:wall-scan-current-direction}
\end{equation}
apart from the smaller self-consistent change in $B_0$. Lowering wall loading lowers
the current demanded by the selected regression. It also lowers beta directly through
Eq.~\eqref{eq:wall-scan-pressure-density}, while the bootstrap-matched scan also changes $a$. Its combined geometry, field and
current changes improve the kink ratio; increasing $R_0$ alone would lower $q_*$ at
fixed $a$, $B_0$ and $I$.

With fixed output,
$P_{\rm CD}$ is fixed. As $W_n$ falls, $R_0n\propto W_n^{-1/2}$ grows, which tends to
reduce the externally driven current in the selected efficiency model. Yet
$I_{\rm req}$ falls more rapidly in this family; the required bootstrap share still
decreases after all quantities are recalculated. The scan therefore recalculates external drive at each trial point.

\section*{102. A spherical tokamak changes the aspect ratio}
Define the plasma aspect ratio by
\begin{equation}
 A\equiv\frac{R_0}{a}.
 \label{eq:aspect-ratio-definition}
\end{equation}
A conventional tokamak has $A$ of order $3$--$4$ or larger. A low-aspect-ratio
tokamak, commonly called a spherical tokamak (ST), has $A$ of order $1$--$2$.
The word spherical describes the compact toroidal shape; the plasma remains a torus,
not a filled sphere. It still needs TF field, poloidal field, equilibrium control,
heating, exhaust, a vacuum boundary, and a nuclear island.

At a fixed fusion mission and wall-loading product $R_0a=C_{\rm wall}$, the two
geometric variables can instead be written in terms of $A$:
\begin{equation}
 R_0=(AC_{\rm wall})^{1/2},\qquad
 a=\left(\frac{C_{\rm wall}}A\right)^{1/2}.
 \label{eq:aspect-ratio-wall-geometry}
\end{equation}
Lowering $A$ widens the cross-section and brings its centre closer to the axis.
The inboard gap shrinks even though the plasma minor radius grows.

\designconsequence{Lower aspect ratio changes field variation, trapped orbits and available
radial space together. We first trace its effect on the two current constraints.}

\samplefigure{\includegraphics[width=.98\linewidth]{figures/final/figS_aspect_ratio_geometry.pdf}}{At fixed $R_0a$, lower aspect ratio widens the plasma cross-section and reduces the inboard gap. The two shapes have the same wall-area scaling at fixed elongation.\label{fig:figS_aspect_ratio_geometry}}

\section*{103. Low aspect ratio strengthens toroidal field variation and trapping}
The toroidal-field relation remains
\[
 B_\varphi(R)R=B_0R_0.
\]
At the inboard and outboard plasma edges, $R=R_0-a$ and $R=R_0+a$. Hence
\begin{equation}
 \frac{B_{\rm out}}{B_{\rm in}}
 =\frac{R_0-a}{R_0+a}
 =\frac{A-1}{A+1}.
 \label{eq:aspect-ratio-field-variation}
\end{equation}
When $A$ falls, this ratio falls: the inboard field becomes much stronger than the
outboard field. The mirror argument from \S36 then says that a larger fraction of
particles can be trapped. For isotropic velocity directions evaluated at the minimum
field, the mirror estimate gives
\begin{equation}
 f_t\simeq
 \left(1-\frac{B_{\min}}{B_{\max}}\right)^{1/2}
 \simeq\left(\frac{2}{A+1}\right)^{1/2}.
 \label{eq:aspect-ratio-trapped-fraction}
\end{equation}
For orientation, this expression gives $f_t\simeq0.63$ at $A=4$ and
$f_t\simeq0.89$ at $A=1.5$.

Trapped and passing particles exchange momentum through collisions, providing a
route from radial pressure gradients to bootstrap current. A larger trapped
population can strengthen that response; its magnitude also depends on profiles
and collisionality.

\samplemodel{This is the local mirror-trapping estimate from toroidal-field geometry
and magnetic-moment conservation. A finite-aspect-ratio kinetic closure supplies the
ST bootstrap fraction in place of the large-$A$ model used in \S\S67--70.}

\section*{104. Aspect ratio also changes the kink--beta coupling}
The shaped edge safety-factor estimate was
\begin{equation}
 q_*=
\frac{2\pi a^2B_0}{\mu_0R_0I}
\left(\frac{1+\kappa^2}{2}\right)
=\frac{2\pi}{\mu_0}
\left(\frac{1+\kappa^2}{2}\right)
\frac{aB_0}{AI}.
 \label{eq:aspect-ratio-q}
\end{equation}
Normalized beta contains the same dimensional group $aB_0/I$:
\begin{equation}
 \beta_N\equiv
\beta(\%)\frac{aB_0}{I_{\rm MA}}.
 \label{eq:aspect-ratio-beta-n}
\end{equation}
Here $I_{\rm MA}=I/(10^6\,\mathrm A)$ and $\beta(\%)=100\beta$. Substitute
\[
 \frac{aB_0}{I}=\frac{\beta_N}{10^8\beta}
\]
into Eq.~\eqref{eq:aspect-ratio-q} to obtain
\begin{equation}
 q_*=
\frac{0.05}{A}
\left(\frac{1+\kappa^2}{2}\right)
\frac{\beta_N}{\beta}.
 \label{eq:aspect-ratio-q-beta}
\end{equation}
The numerical $0.05$ comes only from $\mu_0=4\pi\times10^{-7}$ and the conventional
percent--MA units in $\beta_N$.

At fixed $\beta$, $\beta_N$ and shape, this relation makes $q_*$ increase as $A$
decreases. Equivalently, at fixed $q_*$ and $\beta_N$, lower $A$ permits larger
$\beta$ within the estimate. This suggests a geometric route to easing the
kink--pressure coupling.

\samplemodel{The shaped edge estimate motivates the direction of the comparison.
The allowable $\beta_N$ and kink boundary require a finite-$A$ equilibrium and
stability assessment with specified profiles and wall response.}

\section*{106. Applying the two current tests to an ST}
Freidberg's conventional reference failed two current-centred tests:
\[
 {\cal R}_{K}=\frac{q_K}{q_*}>1,\qquad
 {\cal R}_{B}=\frac{1-I_{\rm CD}/I}{f_{\rm NC}}>1.
\]
The first says that the confinement-demanded current leaves too little safety factor;
the second says that the calculated self-current is smaller than the self-current
share required after permitted external current drive is subtracted. For the
conventional reference, \S\S64 and 70 obtained $1.28$ and $1.91$, respectively.

The ST test keeps the accounting and uses geometry-appropriate inputs:
\[
 {\cal R}_{K,\rm ST}=\frac{q_{K,\rm ST}}{q_{*,\rm ST}},\qquad
 {\cal R}_{B,\rm ST}=\frac{1-I_{\rm CD,allow}/I_{\rm ST}}{f_{\rm BS,ST}}.
\]
Here $q_{K,\rm ST}$ is an ST-specific kink boundary, $f_{\rm BS,ST}$ is obtained
from an ST finite-aspect-ratio bootstrap model, and $I_{\rm CD,allow}$ is the
external current that the plant can afford to supply under an independently stated
power and efficiency constraint. The allowance comes from a power budget; the
bootstrap fraction comes from the plasma model. Their independent origins make
the ratio a test.

\designconsequence{The ST case needs both a safety-factor boundary and a calculated
bootstrap share. The latter determines how much affordable external drive is required.}

\section*{107. The finite-aspect-ratio bootstrap model is declared}
Menard \emph{et al.} use a low-aspect-ratio equilibrium-informed bootstrap closure
of the form
\begin{equation}
 f_{\rm BS}=C_{\rm BS}\sqrt{\epsilon}\,\beta_{\rm pol,th},
 \qquad \epsilon\equiv\frac a{R_0}=\frac1A,
 \label{eq:menard-bootstrap-scaling}
\end{equation}
where $\beta_{\rm pol,th}$ is the thermal poloidal beta and $C_{\rm BS}$ is a
profile/safety-factor coefficient. Their stated parameterization is
\begin{equation}
 C_{\rm BS}=
 \max\!\left(1.2-0.2\frac{q_*}{q_{\min}},\,0.6\right).
 \label{eq:menard-bootstrap-coefficient}
\end{equation}
Here $q_{\min}$ is the minimum safety factor of the assumed equilibrium/current
profile. It is an input to this finite-$A$ closure, not a new universal constant.
\footnote{J. E. Menard \emph{et al.}, cited in \S105, Sec.~2.1.5,
Eqs. and surrounding text on bootstrap/current-drive modeling. They state that this
form is consistent with low-aspect-ratio equilibrium calculations; it is not
deduced from Maxwell's equations alone.}

The definition of poloidal beta makes the physical content visible:
\[
 \beta_{\rm pol,th}\equiv\frac{2\mu_0\langle p_{\rm th}\rangle}{B_{\rm pol}^2}.
\]
It increases when thermal pressure rises relative to the poloidal magnetic field.
Equation~\eqref{eq:menard-bootstrap-scaling} then combines the pressure-gradient
source of bootstrap current with the low-$A$ orbit geometry through
$\sqrt{\epsilon}$. This is the finite-$A$ replacement for Freidberg's
large-aspect-ratio local integral in \S\S67--70.

\samplemodel{The coefficient and equilibrium-derived poloidal beta define the
finite-$A$ closure used in this comparison. Its profile, safety-factor and regime
assumptions accompany the predicted bootstrap fraction.}

 I_{\rm CD,needed}=(1-f_{\rm BS})I\simeq0.24I

\section*{112. Low aspect ratio introduces a centre-stack space requirement}
Lowering $A$ also brings the plasma nearer the symmetry axis and increases the
inboard toroidal field. The centre stack is the central column carrying the
inboard TF current and its support, cooling and insulation, with any allocated
solenoid. Around it, a D--T machine needs nuclear protection and, where assigned, breeding.

The available space must accommodate these functions at the field and current
demanded by the plasma. This is the next engineering test, alongside the
outstanding current-drive affordability condition.

\section*{113. The same geometry takes radial space away from the centre stack}
After reserving plasma radius $a$ and inboard nuclear build $b$, the outer radius
available to the central column is
\begin{equation}
 R_{\rm cs}=R_0-a-b
=R_0\left(1-\frac1A\right)-b.
 \label{eq:centre-stack-radius}
\end{equation}
For an ideal axisymmetric TF system, evaluate the field just outside the enclosed
central TF current. The $BR=\mathrm{constant}$ relation gives
\begin{equation}
 B_{\rm cs}=\frac{B_0R_0}{R_{\rm cs}},\qquad
 B_0=B_{\rm cs}
\left[1-\frac1A-\frac{b}{R_0}\right].
 \label{eq:centre-stack-field}
\end{equation}
Here $R_{\rm cs}$ is the column's outer envelope radius, the same quantity called
$R_{\rm env}$ in the area scan below. Conductor, support, insulation, cooling and
any solenoid are allocated inside this envelope. This convention counts the column area once.

\samplemodel{$B_{\rm cs}$ is the field at an exterior path enclosing the TF current.
Inside the winding region, Ampere's law uses the current enclosed by each path;
a peak field on conductor requires the winding geometry.}

As $A$ approaches one, $R_0(1-1/A)$ shrinks. Preserving nuclear build and column
area then requires a larger $R_0$ or a changed allocation and operating target.

\designconsequence{Low aspect ratio reduces the column envelope. The required field
and current must fit the remaining radius together with the column's other functions.}

\section*{114. HTS raises the usable field and current-density range}
Equation~\eqref{eq:centre-stack-field} explains why HTS becomes especially valuable in
a low-aspect-ratio branch. For fixed desired $B_0$, reducing $A$ raises the required
central-column field $B_{\rm cs}$. A conductor with credible operating current
margin at higher field can make a smaller or more capable centre stack possible.
It may therefore preserve some of the low-$A$ gain that an ordinary conductor
field ceiling would otherwise erase.

The same field also creates magnetic loading:
\[
 p_B=\frac{B_{\rm cs}^2}{2\mu_0}.
\]
The column must carry this load while remaining cooled, insulated, protected and
maintainable in a neutron environment. Higher allowable conductor field can preserve
an operating option, while the accompanying force increases support demand.

\designconsequence{HTS expands the conductor's operating range. The centre-stack
check must include the area and loads of the complete column.}

\section*{115. The coupled calculations needed for an ST plant}
A consistent ST plant case needs the following calculations at the same state:
\begin{enumerate}
\item finite-$A$ equilibrium and stability, including profiles and wall response;
\item bootstrap and external current drive with an independent electricity budget;
\item transport and confinement applicable to the intended ST regime;
\item column area, stress, current-density and protection allowances;
\item neutron shielding, breeding, heat exhaust and component lifetime.
\end{enumerate}
The following bounds isolate the column's electromagnetic and geometric
requirements. They reveal which field, material or space allocation causes a proposed column to fail.

\section*{116. Ampere's law fixes the central TF current requirement}
The toroidal field outside an axis-enclosing current path obeys
\[
 B_\varphi(R)=\frac{\mu_0NI}{2\pi R}.
\]
Evaluate it at the plasma centre:
\begin{equation}
 NI=\frac{2\pi R_0B_0}{\mu_0}.
 \label{eq:centre-stack-ampere-turns}
\end{equation}
The required ampere-turns depend on $R_0B_0$. Moving the current inward raises
the local field while leaving that total current requirement unchanged.
A narrower column must therefore carry the same ampere-turns through less area.

Let $J_{\rm wp,cs}$ be the allowed current per unit winding-pack area. It includes
internal conductor, stabilizer, insulation, cooling and any support already
allocated within the pack. Separate column support and service areas are counted outside it.

The current-carrying cross-sectional area has the necessary lower bound
\begin{equation}
 A_{\rm wp,cs}\ge
\frac{NI}{J_{\rm wp,cs}}
=\frac{2\pi R_0B_0}{\mu_0J_{\rm wp,cs}}.
 \label{eq:centre-stack-winding-area}
\end{equation}
The result is a minimum winding-pack area. The column must provide that area as
well as its separately allocated support and services.

\section*{117. A first necessary radius inequality for the centre stack}
If the entire envelope were available to the winding pack, its circular area would require
\[
 \pi R_{\rm cs}^2\ge A_{\rm wp,cs}.
\]
Substitute Eq.~\eqref{eq:centre-stack-winding-area}:
\begin{equation}
 R_{\rm cs}\ge
\left(\frac{2R_0B_0}{\mu_0J_{\rm wp,cs}}\right)^{1/2}.
 \label{eq:centre-stack-radius-current-bound}
\end{equation}
Allocating the other column functions strengthens the requirement:
\begin{equation}
 \pi R_{\rm cs}^2\ge
 A_{\rm wp,cs}+A_{\rm str}+A_{\rm ins/cool}+A_{\rm sol}
+A_{\rm margin},
 \label{eq:centre-stack-area-ledger}
\end{equation}
where $A_{\rm str}$ is support outside the winding pack, $A_{\rm ins/cool}$
covers electrical and thermal services outside the pack, $A_{\rm sol}$ is any
central-solenoid allocation, and the final term represents manufacturing,
protection, and operational margin.

Each area is counted once. Reducing $A$ shrinks the available envelope while
field, materials and operating functions set the required areas.

\designconsequence{The column must satisfy an explicit area ledger. A current-carrying
pack that uses another function's allocation has not closed the geometry.}

\section*{118. Central field sets a structural load scale}
At the centre-stack radius, the local magnetic-pressure scale is
\[
 p_{B,\rm cs}=\frac{B_{\rm cs}^2}{2\mu_0}.
\]
If a representative structural load path of area $A_{\rm load}$ is carried by
material limited to $\sigma_{\rm allow}$, force balance requires
\begin{equation}
 A_{\rm str}\gtrsim
\frac{p_{B,\rm cs}}{\sigma_{\rm allow}}A_{\rm load}.
 \label{eq:centre-stack-structure-bound}
\end{equation}
The detailed geometry determines $A_{\rm load}$ and the actual stress distribution;
the quadratic field dependence is already inescapable. As low $A$ reduces
$R_{\rm cs}$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:centre-stack-field} pushes $B_{\rm cs}$ upward for a
fixed $B_0$. The winding pack and its supporting structure then compete for the same shrinking area.

\samplemodel{This is a load-path scaling. Detailed sizing requires the three-dimensional
force distribution, superconductor strain limits, cyclic loads, protection and
neutron-degraded material properties. HTS can improve current-carrying margin while
higher operating field increases the load to be supported.}

\section*{119. The transformer also loses flux swing as the column shrinks}
The transformer argument from \S11 gives a second, independent centre-stack
constraint. A solenoid of effective radius $R_{\rm sol}$ changes linked magnetic
flux by the scale
\begin{equation}
 \Delta\Psi_{\rm sol}\sim\pi R_{\rm sol}^2\Delta B_{\rm sol}.
 \label{eq:centre-stack-flux-swing}
\end{equation}
Faraday's law converts this flux swing into the volt-seconds available to initiate
and ramp plasma current. Since $R_{\rm sol}$ must fit inside the same central
column, $R_{\rm sol}<R_{\rm cs}$, at fixed usable $\Delta B_{\rm sol}$ the flux
capacity falls with the square of the allocated solenoid radius.

This flux change supplies the volt-seconds used to initiate, ramp and inductively
sustain plasma current. A chosen pulse needs a sufficient flux budget.
Non-inductive start-up can release solenoid area, while creating a requirement
for another means of initiating current, such as radio-frequency drive,
neutral beams or helicity injection.

\designconsequence{Reducing solenoid area reduces available inductive flux. Any space
saved must be paired with a specified start-up and current-sustainment route.}

\section*{120. The central-stack test is now ready to be made quantitative}
The equations above turn the phrase centre-stack problem into five checkable questions:
\begin{enumerate}
\item What $B_0$, $R_0$, and $A$ are demanded by the plasma mission?
\item Does Eq.~\eqref{eq:centre-stack-field} leave positive inboard radius after the required nuclear build $b$?
\item Does the available $\pi R_{\rm cs}^2$ exceed the full area ledger in Eq.~\eqref{eq:centre-stack-area-ledger}?
\item Can the allocated support carry the magnetic load within its stress limit and the superconductor strain limit?
\item If solenoid area is reduced, what explicit non-inductive start-up and current maintenance system replaces its flux swing?
\end{enumerate}
The remaining calculation varies the declared field, radius, nuclear build,
winding-pack current density and area allocation. It begins with a necessary area
test before adding the other requirements.

\section*{121. A published ST study shows why the ledger must be literal}
A published allocation gives the radial budget a concrete scale. Menard \emph{et al.}
examined an illustrative FNSF configuration with $A=2$, $R_0=1.87\,\mathrm m$,
and a $30\,\mathrm{cm}$ centre-column radius. Their stated inboard nuclear
protection included approximately $60\,\mathrm{cm}$ of water-cooled tungsten-carbide
shielding; the corresponding radial picture also includes a $2\,\mathrm{cm}$
first wall. Their magnet assessment used a $70\,\mathrm{MA\,m^{-2}}$
winding-pack current-density assumption and imposed both support-stress and
superconducting-strain constraints.\footnote{Menard \emph{et al.}, cited in \S105,
Secs.~2.1 and 5.1, especially their Figs.~46--47. The numbers in this paragraph
are inputs and results of that particular FNSF study, not universal requirements.}

The first calculation needs no plasma model. At $A=2$,
\[
 a=\frac{R_0}{A}=0.935\,\mathrm m,\qquad R_0-a=0.935\,\mathrm m.
\]
Temporarily subtract only the stated shield and first-wall thickness:
\begin{equation}
 0.935-0.600-0.020=0.315\,\mathrm m.
 \label{eq:menard-geometry-check}
\end{equation}
This is close to the study's $0.30\,\mathrm m$ column radius. The subtraction is a
rounded envelope check; it does not resolve the internal allocations or establish usable clearance.

\designconsequence{The shield and first wall consume most of this example's inboard
radius. Their mission-dependent thickness must be reserved before sizing the column.}

\section*{122. Nuclear functions set the space available to the column}
D--T neutrons carry $14.1\,\mathrm{MeV}$ and are uncharged, so magnetic confinement
leaves their paths essentially unaffected. They deposit heat and cause damage in the
first wall, nuclear region and magnets. A D--T plant must also breed replacement
tritium from lithium. These functions determine the inboard build $b$.

The $60\,\mathrm{cm}$ shield in \S121 is a protection choice for that study's mission
and materials. Changing it demands a new neutronics, heating, lifetime, and
tritium-breeding calculation. The same study found that at low aspect ratio the
inboard side can also be important to a tritium-breeding solution.\footnote{Menard
\emph{et al.}, cited in \S105, discuss the inboard shielding and breeding allocation
in their Sec.~5.3. Their conclusion is configuration-specific; the general point
here is only that breeding and shielding claim radial space before the magnet is sized.}

The design sequence follows the energy flow: specify the plasma mission, calculate
neutron protection and breeding requirements, then allocate the remaining column to
TF winding, support and start-up.

\samplefigure{\includegraphics[width=.98\linewidth]{figures/final/figS_st_radial_ledger.pdf}}{Illustrative $A=2$ radial allocation from Menard \emph{et al.} Shield and first wall leave about $0.3\,\mathrm m$ for the column. Its internal winding, support and start-up allocations require a separate design.\label{fig:figS_st_radial_ledger}}

\section*{123. What HTS changes in this ledger}
HTS changes a specific term: the credible current density and operating field margin
of the TF winding pack. In the necessary bound,
\[
 A_{\rm wp,cs}\ge\frac{2\pi R_0B_0}{\mu_0J_{\rm wp,cs}},
\]
raising a credibly usable $J_{\rm wp,cs}$ reduces the minimum current-carrying area
for a prescribed $R_0B_0$. It can therefore create room that a conventional
conductor cannot. In the field relation
\[
 B_{\rm cs}=\frac{B_0R_0}{R_{\rm cs}},
\]
HTS can also make a larger central field technically plausible without immediately
crossing the conductor's critical surface.

Increasing usable $J_{\rm wp,cs}$ reduces the required winding area. Support,
services and start-up retain their own allocations, and higher $B_{\rm cs}$
increases the magnetic-pressure load as $B_{\rm cs}^2$.

The published low-$A$ work gives a concrete architectural direction rather than a
shortcut. A later $A=2$, $R_0=3\,\mathrm m$ HTS pilot-plant concept places all
superconducting poloidal-field coils outside the TF system, uses continuous HTS TF
coils, retains only a small central solenoid, and keeps inboard blanket/shield
functions in the radial build.\footnote{Menard \emph{et al.}, cited in \S105,
Sec.~5.4 and Fig.~53. It is a conditional systems study, not a demonstration that
every one of its performance targets has been experimentally achieved.}
Each choice answers one item in the ledger: outside PF coils avoid consuming
central volume; a small solenoid makes non-inductive start-up more important; HTS
improves the TF field option; blanket and shield remain non-negotiable.

\designconsequence{HTS can reduce winding area or permit higher field. Its benefit
must be evaluated with the column's stress, nuclear protection and start-up allocations.}

\section*{124. What remains after the Freidberg answer}
The finite-$A$ case passes the adopted kink screen and specifies about
$2.6$--$2.9\,\mathrm{MA}$ of external drive for bootstrap closure. Its geometry
also increases the pressure on inboard space. These results connect the plasma
and column calculations without settling the outstanding affordability condition.

The next calculation must therefore have two ledgers, evaluated together:
\[
\begin{array}{c|c}
\text{finite-$A$ plasma ledger} & \text{centre-stack reactor ledger}\\ \hline
q,\ \beta_N,\ \text{equilibrium/stability} &
b,\ R_{\rm cs},\ A_{\rm wp},\ A_{\rm str}\\
\text{bootstrap and driven current} &
\text{shield, breeding, stress, start-up}\\
\text{transport and exhaust} &
\text{HTS margin, protection, maintenance}
\end{array}
\]
The final teaching scan makes the column's current-area requirement explicit.

\section*{125. A dimensionless necessary test for a central TF winding}
For a first scan, reserve the inboard nuclear build $b$ before allocating the
central column. The outer radius of the column envelope is then
\begin{equation}
 R_{\rm env}=R_0\left(1-\frac1A\right)-b.
 \label{eq:centre-stack-envelope}
\end{equation}
This is the same outer-envelope radius denoted by $R_{\rm cs}$ in \S\S113--120.
Require $R_{\rm env}>0$ before evaluating an area. The allocation within that
circle is specified separately.

Let $f_{\rm wp}$ be the fraction of the envelope's circular cross-sectional area
that the designer has explicitly allocated to the TF winding pack. It records the
area assigned to winding after reserving the other functions.
Equation~\eqref{eq:centre-stack-winding-area} then requires
\[
 \frac{2\pi R_0B_0}{\mu_0J_{\rm wp,cs}}\le f_{\rm wp}\pi R_{\rm env}^2.
\]
After cancelling $\pi$, define the dimensionless winding occupancy
\begin{equation}
 \eta_{\rm wp}\equiv
 \frac{2R_0B_0}
 {\mu_0J_{\rm wp,cs}\left[R_0(1-1/A)-b\right]^2}.
 \label{eq:centre-stack-winding-occupancy}
\end{equation}
The necessary test is simply
\begin{equation}
 \eta_{\rm wp}\le f_{\rm wp}<1.
 \label{eq:centre-stack-winding-test}
\end{equation}
If $\eta_{\rm wp}>1$, even a column made entirely of winding pack cannot carry the
required TF current. If $f_{\rm wp}<\eta_{\rm wp}<1$, a central column exists only
on paper because the current pack has consumed space explicitly promised to the other functions.

\samplemodel{The pack is represented by an effective uniform current density.
This is a necessary current-area screen; field distribution, stress, strain,
protection, nuclear lifetime and manufacturability require additional checks.}

\section*{126. Solving the test exposes a lower-aspect-ratio boundary}
The test can be solved for the smallest aspect ratio permitted by the chosen area
allocation. Take the positive square root of Eq.~\eqref{eq:centre-stack-winding-test}:
\[
 R_0\left(1-\frac1A\right)-b\ge
 \left(\frac{2R_0B_0}{\mu_0J_{\rm wp,cs}f_{\rm wp}}\right)^{1/2}.
\]
Divide by $R_0$ and rearrange:
\begin{equation}
 A\ge A_{\min,\rm wp}\equiv
\left[
1-\frac{b}{R_0}
-\left(\frac{2B_0}
{\mu_0J_{\rm wp,cs}f_{\rm wp}R_0}\right)^{1/2}
\right]^{-1}.
\label{eq:centre-stack-aspect-bound}
\end{equation}
The bracket must be positive. If it is zero or negative, the stated $R_0$, $B_0$,
nuclear build, winding-pack current density, and allocation cannot coexist at any
aspect ratio in this simplified central-column arrangement.

Every trend in this equation has a physical reading:
\begin{itemize}
\item Increasing $B_0$ increases the required ampere-turns and raises the minimum allowed $A$.
\item Increasing $b/R_0$ claims the inboard radius for nuclear function and raises the minimum allowed $A$.
\item Increasing credible $J_{\rm wp,cs}$ or giving the winding pack a larger allocated
fraction $f_{\rm wp}$ lowers the electromagnetic area demand.
\item Increasing $R_0$ helps twice: the nuclear build occupies a smaller fraction
of the radius and the required winding area is less crowded into the column.
\end{itemize}
HTS can improve the usable pack current density; a larger machine can reduce
crowding. Each improvement must retain credible allowances for the other functions.

\section*{127. A transparent teaching scan: field makes the low-$A$ limit move}
Use the deliberately declared teaching inputs
\[
 R_0=3.0\,\mathrm m,\quad b=0.65\,\mathrm m,\quad
 J_{\rm wp,cs}=70\,\mathrm{MA\,m^{-2}},\quad f_{\rm wp}=0.40.
\]
The current-density scale is comparable to an assumption used in the illustrative
Menard study in \S121; the radius, nuclear build, and $40\%$ allocation here are
not a proposed reactor. They merely make the algebra visible.
Substitution in Eq.~\eqref{eq:centre-stack-aspect-bound} gives:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{c c c}
$B_0\;(\mathrm T)$ & $A_{\min,\rm wp}$ & $\eta_{\rm wp}$ at $A=2$\\ \hline
4 & 1.97 & 0.378\\
6 & 2.24 & 0.566\\
8 & 2.54 & 0.755
\end{tabular}
\tablecaption{Necessary winding-area test for the declared teaching inputs.\label{tab:centre-stack-scan}}
\end{center}
At $A=2$, the available envelope radius is
$R_{\rm env}=3(1-1/2)-0.65=0.85\,\mathrm m$. The first row barely clears the
assigned $40\%$ winding allocation. The latter two do not: they require more
winding area than has been allocated even before support, insulation, cooling, or
a solenoid are tested. The table is reproducible from
\texttt{centre\_stack\_ledger.py}; every input is displayed at the top of that file.

The $4\,\mathrm T$ case requires about $38\%$ of the envelope for winding, close
to the assigned $40\%$. At $6$ and $8\,\mathrm T$, the required fractions rise to
about $57\%$ and $76\%$. The current-area requirement alone then exceeds the allocation.

Raising usable winding-pack current density lowers the minimum aspect ratio.
Spending that gain on higher plasma field increases required ampere-turns and
moves the boundary back upward. HTS therefore changes the trade between field
and space; its net benefit depends on the chosen operating point.

\samplescope{The teaching scan reserves a winding fraction but does not size the
remaining support and services or calculate the nuclear build, plasma stability
and exhaust. A passing row meets the current-area requirement only.}

\designconsequence{The reduced model now identifies a measurable requirement:
enough column area to carry the TF current after nuclear protection is reserved.
The next research stage must test that requirement alongside affordable current
drive and an explicit start-up route.}
